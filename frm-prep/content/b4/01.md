## Expected return and standard deviation

The **expected return** of an investment is the probability-weighted average of its possible returns. It is the mean of the distribution, not the most likely outcome: an investment that returns −10% or +30% with equal probability has an expected return of 10%, a result that never occurs.

The **standard deviation** of the return measures how widely outcomes spread around the mean. It is the square root of the variance, and the variance equals the expected squared return minus the squared expected return.

:::formula Mean and standard deviation of a return
$$\E(R)=\sum_{i} p_i R_i \qquad \SD(R)=\sqrt{\E(R^2)-\left[\E(R)\right]^2} \qquad \E(R^2)=\sum_i p_i R_i^2$$
- \(p_i\): probability of outcome \(i\)
- \(R_i\): return in outcome \(i\), in decimal form
:::

:::example Mean and standard deviation from a probability table
A one-year investment has the return distribution below.

| Probability | Return |
|---|---|
| 0.10 | −15% |
| 0.20 | 0% |
| 0.40 | 6% |
| 0.20 | 12% |
| 0.10 | 30% |

1. Expected return: \(0.10(-0.15)+0.20(0)+0.40(0.06)+0.20(0.12)+0.10(0.30)=0.0630\).
2. Expected squared return: \(0.10(0.0225)+0.20(0)+0.40(0.0036)+0.20(0.0144)+0.10(0.09)=0.01557\).
3. Variance: \(0.01557-0.0630^2=0.01557-0.003969=0.011601\).
4. Standard deviation: \(\sqrt{0.011601}=0.1077\).

**Answer:** expected return 6.30%, standard deviation 10.77%.
:::

A risk-free asset, such as a one-year Treasury bill held for one year, has a return standard deviation of zero over that horizon because its return is known in advance.

## Combining two investments

Put a fraction \(w_1\) of wealth in investment 1 and \(w_2 = 1-w_1\) in investment 2. The portfolio's expected return is the weighted average of the two expected returns. Its standard deviation is not a weighted average: it depends on the **correlation** \(\rho\) between the two returns.

:::formula Two-asset portfolio
$$\mu_P = w_1\mu_1 + w_2\mu_2$$
$$\sigma_P=\sqrt{w_1^2\sigma_1^2+w_2^2\sigma_2^2+2\rho\, w_1 w_2\,\sigma_1\sigma_2}$$
- \(\mu_i, \sigma_i\): mean and standard deviation of the return on investment \(i\)
- \(w_i\): fraction of the portfolio in investment \(i\), with \(w_1+w_2=1\)
- \(\rho\): correlation between the two returns
:::

With \(n\) investments, the variance sums the same term over every pair:

:::formula Portfolio of n investments
$$\mu_P=\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i\mu_i \qquad \sigma_P^2=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\rho_{ij}\,w_i w_j\,\sigma_i\sigma_j$$
- \(\rho_{ij}\): correlation between returns \(i\) and \(j\), with \(\rho_{ii}=1\)
:::

### How correlation drives diversification

Diversification comes from the covariance term \(2\rho w_1w_2\sigma_1\sigma_2\).

- With \(\rho = 1\), \(\sigma_P = w_1\sigma_1 + w_2\sigma_2\) exactly, so mixing gives no reduction in risk.
- With \(\rho < 1\), \(\sigma_P\) is below the weighted average of the two standard deviations.
- With \(\rho = -1\), the weight \(w_1 = \sigma_2/(\sigma_1+\sigma_2)\) makes \(\sigma_P = 0\).

The portfolio with the lowest standard deviation puts this weight in investment 1:

:::formula Minimum-variance weight, two assets
$$w_1^{*}=\frac{\sigma_2^2-\rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2}{\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2-2\rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2}$$
:::

:::example Risk-return combinations for two investments
Investment A has \(\mu_A = 6\%\) and \(\sigma_A = 10\%\). Investment B has \(\mu_B = 10\%\) and \(\sigma_B = 20\%\). The correlation is 0.3. For \(w_A = 0.8\):

1. \(\mu_P = 0.8(6\%) + 0.2(10\%) = 6.8\%\).
2. \(\sigma_P^2 = 0.8^2(0.10)^2 + 0.2^2(0.20)^2 + 2(0.3)(0.8)(0.2)(0.10)(0.20) = 0.0064 + 0.0016 + 0.00192 = 0.00992\).
3. \(\sigma_P = \sqrt{0.00992} = 9.96\%\).

Repeating the calculation for other weights:

| \(w_A\) | \(w_B\) | \(\mu_P\) | \(\sigma_P\) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | 0.0 | 6.0% | 10.00% |
| 0.8 | 0.2 | 6.8% | 9.96% |
| 0.6 | 0.4 | 7.6% | 11.35% |
| 0.4 | 0.6 | 8.4% | 13.74% |
| 0.2 | 0.8 | 9.2% | 16.71% |
| 0.0 | 1.0 | 10.0% | 20.00% |

Holding only A is worse than the 80/20 mix: 6.0% expected return at 10.00% risk against 6.8% at 9.96%. The minimum-variance weight is \(w_A^{*} = (0.04 - 0.006)/(0.01 + 0.04 - 0.012) = 0.895\), which gives \(\sigma_P = 9.79\%\) and \(\mu_P = 6.42\%\).
:::

## The efficient frontier

One portfolio **dominates** another when it has a higher expected return and no higher standard deviation, or a lower standard deviation and no lower expected return. An investor who judges portfolios only by mean and standard deviation never holds a dominated portfolio. The **efficient frontier** is the set of portfolios that no other portfolio dominates: for each level of standard deviation, the highest attainable expected return.

In the two-asset example, the 100% A portfolio is dominated. The combinations from the minimum-variance portfolio (89.5% A) up to 100% B form the efficient frontier for those two assets.

A third asset can be combined with every mix of the first two, which moves the frontier up and to the left; a fourth extends it again. Using all risky assets gives the **efficient frontier of risky investments**. Portfolios on or below and to the right of it can be built. Portfolios above and to the left of it cannot.

### Adding a risk-free asset

Combining a risk-free asset (return \(R_F\), standard deviation zero) with a risky portfolio M produces a straight line in mean–standard deviation space, because the risk-free asset contributes no variance and no covariance.

:::formula Risk-free asset combined with risky portfolio M
$$\mu_P = R_F + \frac{\mu_M - R_F}{\sigma_M}\,\sigma_P \qquad \sigma_P = w_M\,\sigma_M$$
- \(w_M\): fraction of wealth in M; the remaining \(1 - w_M\) is lent at \(R_F\) if positive and borrowed at \(R_F\) if negative
- \((\mu_M - R_F)/\sigma_M\): extra expected return per unit of standard deviation, the slope of the line
:::

The steepest attainable line touches the risky frontier at one tangency portfolio, and that line becomes the new efficient frontier. Every investor then holds the same risky portfolio and sets total risk by lending (a point between \(R_F\) and M) or borrowing (a point beyond M). If all investors hold the same risky portfolio, it must contain every risky asset in proportion to its market value, so it is the **market portfolio**. This result leads to the capital asset pricing model, covered in [Book 1, Chapter 5](#/b1-05).

The result rests on assumptions that hold only approximately:

- Investors care only about the mean and standard deviation of portfolio returns.
- All investors use the same estimates of means, standard deviations, and correlations.
- All investors can borrow and lend at the same risk-free rate.
- There are no taxes or transaction costs.

:::example Choosing a point on the line
The risk-free rate is 3%. The market portfolio has \(\mu_M = 9\%\) and \(\sigma_M = 15\%\).

1. Slope: \((9\% - 3\%)/15\% = 0.40\).
2. Target standard deviation 10%: \(w_M = 10/15 = 0.667\), with 33.3% lent at 3%. Expected return \(= 3\% + 0.40 \times 10\% = 7.0\%\).
3. Target expected return 12%: \(w_M = (12\% - 3\%)/(9\% - 3\%) = 1.5\), with an amount equal to 50% of wealth borrowed at 3%. Standard deviation \(= 1.5 \times 15\% = 22.5\%\).
:::

## Normal distributions and real returns

A **normal distribution** is fully described by its mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\). The probability that a value falls between \(a\) and \(b\) is the area under the density between them, which equals the cumulative probability at \(b\) minus the cumulative probability at \(a\).

:::formula Normal density and z-score
$$f(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)} \qquad z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma} \qquad P(X\le x)=\Phi(z)$$
- \(\Phi\): cumulative distribution function of the standard normal (mean 0, standard deviation 1), tabulated on the [normal table page](#/reference)
:::

:::example Normal probabilities with z-scores
Daily profit \(X\) is normal with mean 1 and standard deviation 4 (USD millions).

1. \(P(X < -3)\): \(z = (-3-1)/4 = -1\), and \(\Phi(-1) = 1 - 0.8413 = 0.1587\).
2. \(P(X < 5)\): \(z = (5-1)/4 = 1\), and \(\Phi(1) = 0.8413\).
3. \(P(-3 < X < 5) = 0.8413 - 0.1587 = 0.6827\).
:::

A normal model is convenient for portfolios: once the portfolio mean and standard deviation are computed from the formulas above, every probability follows. Returns on most financial assets are not normal. Compared with a normal distribution that has the same standard deviation, daily equity returns are:

- **More peaked**: very small moves occur more often.
- **Fat-tailed**: very large moves occur more often.
- **Thinner in the shoulders**: moderate moves occur less often.

The reading shows this with daily S&P 500 changes from 2000 to 2019, when the daily standard deviation was 1.187%:

| Daily move larger than | Share of days observed | Normal prediction |
|---|---|---|
| 1 SD | 21.05% | 31.73% |
| 2 SD | 5.13% | 4.55% |
| 3 SD | 1.64% | 0.27% |
| 4 SD | 0.64% | 0.01% |
| 5 SD | 0.30% | 0.00% |
| 6 SD | 0.12% | 0.00% |

Moves beyond three standard deviations occurred six times as often as the normal distribution predicts. A risk model that assumes normality, even with the correct standard deviation, understates the probability of extreme losses, and the understatement grows as the confidence level rises.

## Value-at-risk (VaR)

Standard deviation describes spread around the mean but says nothing specific about the tail. **Value-at-risk (VaR)** is a tail measure: the loss over a stated **time horizon** that is exceeded only with probability \(1 - X\), where \(X\) is the **confidence level**. VaR is the \(X\) percentile of the loss distribution.

A one-day 99% VaR of USD 10 million means a loss larger than USD 10 million is expected on 1% of days, or about 2.5 days in a 250-day trading year.

| Use | Horizon | Measure |
|---|---|---|
| Basel market risk capital (1996 rules) | 10 days | 99% VaR |
| Basel II credit risk capital | 1 year | 99.9% VaR |
| Fundamental Review of the Trading Book (FRTB) | 10 days, longer for less liquid risk factors | 97.5% expected shortfall |

VaR can be read from a loss distribution (losses positive), where it is the \(X\) percentile, or from a profit or return distribution, where it is the \(1-X\) percentile with its sign reversed. If the expected profit is large relative to the standard deviation, VaR can be negative: even the \(X\) percentile outcome is a gain.

### VaR when outcomes are normal

:::formula Normal VaR
$$\VaR_X = \mu_L + z_X\,\sigma = -\mu_{P\&L} + z_X\,\sigma$$
- \(\mu_L\): mean loss over the horizon; \(\mu_{P\&L} = -\mu_L\) is the mean profit
- \(\sigma\): standard deviation of profit (equal to that of the loss) over the horizon
- \(z_X\): standard normal value with probability \(1 - X\) above it: 1.645 at 95%, 2.326 at 99%, 3.090 at 99.9%
:::

:::example Normal VaR with a positive expected profit
A portfolio's one-day profit is normal with mean USD 0.2 million and standard deviation USD 2 million.

1. 95% VaR: \(-0.2 + 1.645 \times 2 = 3.090\), or USD 3.09 million.
2. 99% VaR: \(-0.2 + 2.326 \times 2 = 4.452\), or USD 4.45 million.
:::

:::error Adding the mean instead of subtracting it
An expected profit lowers VaR. With a mean profit of USD 0.2 million, 99% VaR is \(4.652 - 0.2 = 4.452\) million. Writing \(4.652 + 0.2 = 4.852\) treats the expected profit as an expected loss.
:::

### VaR from discrete and uniform distributions

For a discrete loss distribution, order the outcomes from best to worst and accumulate their probabilities. VaR is the loss at which the cumulative probability first reaches \(X\).

:::example Discrete loss distribution
A one-year exposure has the loss distribution below; a negative loss is a gain.

| Loss (USD m) | Probability | Cumulative probability |
|---|---|---|
| −2 | 80.0% | 80.0% |
| 1 | 14.0% | 94.0% |
| 4 | 4.5% | 98.5% |
| 10 | 1.5% | 100.0% |

1. 95% VaR: 95% falls in the band from 94.0% to 98.5%, which belongs to a loss of 4. VaR is USD 4 million.
2. 99% VaR: 99% falls in the band from 98.5% to 100%. VaR is USD 10 million.
3. At exactly 98.5% confidence, both 4 and 10 qualify. One convention takes the average, USD 7 million.
:::

In a **uniform** distribution every value in a range is equally likely, so the tail probability grows in proportion to the distance from the worst outcome.

:::example Uniform profit distribution
One-year profit is equally likely to take any value from a loss of 25 to a gain of 75 (USD millions), a range 100 wide.

1. The worst 5% of outcomes span \(0.05 \times 100 = 5\): losses from 20 to 25. 95% VaR is 20.
2. The worst 1% span 1: losses from 24 to 25. 99% VaR is 24.
:::

### Choosing the horizon

When daily changes are independent and identically distributed, variances add across days. The standard deviation over \(T\) days therefore grows with \(\sqrt{T}\), while the mean grows with \(T\).

:::formula Square-root-of-time rule
$$\sigma_T=\sigma_1\sqrt{T} \qquad \mu_T=\mu_1\,T \qquad \VaR_T=\VaR_1\sqrt{T}\ \text{ when }\mu_1=0$$
- \(\sigma_1, \mu_1\): one-day standard deviation and mean
- \(T\): horizon in trading days
:::

:::example Ten-day VaR from one-day inputs
Daily profit has mean zero and standard deviation USD 1.2 million, and days are independent.

1. Ten-day standard deviation: \(1.2\sqrt{10} = 3.795\).
2. Ten-day 99% VaR: \(2.326 \times 3.795 = 8.827\), or USD 8.83 million.
3. The same result from the one-day VaR: \(2.326 \times 1.2 = 2.791\), and \(2.791 \times \sqrt{10} = 8.827\).
:::

:::error Scaling by T instead of the square root of T
Multiplying the one-day VaR of USD 2.79 million by 10 gives USD 27.9 million, more than three times the correct USD 8.83 million. Only the mean scales with \(T\).
:::

The rule assumes independent daily changes and a portfolio held unchanged over the horizon. Positive autocorrelation in daily returns makes \(\sqrt{T}\) scaling understate multi-day risk. A desk that cuts positions after losses has less multi-day risk than the unchanged-portfolio calculation shows.

### Limitations of VaR

- **Silent beyond the cutoff.** VaR reports one percentile and ignores the size of losses past it. Two portfolios can both have a 99% VaR of USD 20 million while the first can lose at most USD 30 million and the second would most likely lose USD 100 million in its worst 1% of outcomes.
- **Open to gaming.** A trader who sells credit protection on a company with a 0.9% default probability, losing USD 100 million if it defaults, has a stand-alone 99% VaR at or below zero: the default loss sits entirely inside the 1% tail that VaR does not measure.
- **Not subadditive.** Combining two positions can produce a VaR larger than the sum of their separate VaRs, which contradicts diversification (worked example below).
- **Model-dependent.** A normal assumption understates tail probabilities for fat-tailed returns, and the error is largest at high confidence levels such as 99.9%.
- **Static.** VaR assumes the portfolio is held unchanged over the horizon and ignores the cost of unwinding positions in illiquid markets.

## Expected shortfall (ES)

**Expected shortfall (ES)** is the expected loss over the horizon conditional on the loss exceeding the \(X\) percentile. It is also called **conditional VaR (C-VaR)** or **tail loss**. VaR is the loss at the edge of the tail; ES is the average loss inside the tail. Every loss in the tail is at least as large as VaR, so ES is always greater than or equal to VaR at the same confidence level.

:::formula Expected shortfall when losses are normal
$$\ES_X=\mu_L+\sigma\,\frac{e^{-z_X^2/2}}{(1-X)\sqrt{2\pi}}=\mu_L+\sigma\,\frac{\phi(z_X)}{1-X}$$
- \(\phi(z_X)\): standard normal density evaluated at \(z_X\)
- Multiples of \(\sigma\) when \(\mu_L = 0\): 2.063 at 95%, 2.338 at 97.5%, 2.665 at 99%
:::

At 97.5%, ES equals 2.338 standard deviations, close to 99% VaR at 2.326. Under normality, FRTB's switch from 99% VaR to 97.5% ES keeps capital at about the same level while making it respond to the size of tail losses.

:::example Normal VaR and ES side by side
Daily profit is normal with mean zero and standard deviation USD 2 million.

1. 99% VaR: \(2.326 \times 2 = 4.652\), or USD 4.65 million.
2. \(\phi(2.326) = e^{-2.326^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi} = 0.06686/2.5066 = 0.02667\).
3. 99% ES: \(2 \times 0.02667/0.01 = 5.334\), or USD 5.33 million.
4. 97.5% ES: \(2.338 \times 2 = 4.676\), or USD 4.68 million.
:::

For a discrete distribution, ES averages the losses in the worst \(1 - X\) of probability. When the VaR outcome's probability straddles the cutoff, only the part needed to fill the tail is included.

:::example ES from the discrete and uniform distributions above
Discrete distribution (loss 10 with 1.5%, loss 4 with 4.5%):

1. The 5% tail holds all 1.5% at a loss of 10 and 3.5% of the 4.5% at a loss of 4.
2. 95% ES \(= (0.015 \times 10 + 0.035 \times 4)/0.05 = (0.15 + 0.14)/0.05 = 5.8\), or USD 5.8 million, compared with a 95% VaR of 4.
3. The 1% tail holds only the loss of 10, so 99% ES is USD 10 million, equal to 99% VaR.

Uniform distribution (loss of 25 to gain of 75): losses in the worst 5% are spread evenly from 20 to 25, so 95% ES is their midpoint, 22.5. The worst 1% runs from 24 to 25, so 99% ES is 24.5.
:::

| | VaR | Expected shortfall |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Loss at the \(X\) percentile | Average loss beyond the \(X\) percentile |
| Reflects the size of losses beyond the cutoff | No | Yes |
| Subadditive | Not always | Always |
| Size at the same \(X\) | Lower than or equal to ES | Higher than or equal to VaR |
| Regulatory use | Basel 1996 market risk, Basel II credit risk | FRTB market risk at 97.5% |

## Coherent risk measures

Artzner, Delbaen, Eber, and Heath (1999) set out four properties that a risk measure used to set capital should satisfy. A measure with all four is **coherent**. In the table, \(\rho(P)\) is the risk measure of portfolio \(P\), read as the capital it requires.

| Property | Condition | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Monotonicity** | If \(P_1\) produces a worse result than \(P_2\) in every state, \(\rho(P_1) \ge \rho(P_2)\) | A portfolio that always does worse needs at least as much capital |
| **Translation invariance** | \(\rho(P + K) = \rho(P) - K\) for cash \(K\) | Adding cash reduces required capital one-for-one, because cash absorbs losses |
| **Homogeneity** | \(\rho(\lambda P) = \lambda\,\rho(P)\) for \(\lambda > 0\) | Scaling every position by \(\lambda\) scales required capital by \(\lambda\) |
| **Subadditivity** | \(\rho(P_1 + P_2) \le \rho(P_1) + \rho(P_2)\) | Merging portfolios never raises total required capital, so diversification is rewarded |

Homogeneity can fail for very large positions. A position 100 times larger can be more than 100 times as risky because it takes longer to unwind.

ES satisfies all four properties. VaR satisfies the first three but can violate subadditivity, so VaR is not coherent. When portfolio outcomes are jointly normal, VaR is subadditive; violations come from skewed, lumpy loss distributions such as those created by defaults.

### Why VaR is not coherent: a worked example

:::example Two loans that break subadditivity
Each of two independent loans loses USD 10 million with probability 3% and nothing otherwise. The confidence level is 95%.

1. Each loan alone: the 3% probability of a loss is below 5%, so 95% VaR is 0. The 5% tail holds 3% at a loss of 10 and 2% at 0, so 95% ES is \((0.03 \times 10)/0.05 = 6\).
2. The two loans together:

| Loss (USD m) | Probability | Cumulative |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | \(0.97^2 = 94.09\%\) | 94.09% |
| 10 | \(2 \times 0.03 \times 0.97 = 5.82\%\) | 99.91% |
| 20 | \(0.03^2 = 0.09\%\) | 100.00% |

3. Portfolio 95% VaR: 95% falls in the 94.09% to 99.91% band, so VaR is 10. The sum of the stand-alone VaRs is \(0 + 0 = 0\), so subadditivity fails.
4. Portfolio 95% ES: the 5% tail holds 0.09% at 20 and 4.91% at 10, so ES \(= (0.0009 \times 20 + 0.0491 \times 10)/0.05 = 10.18\). That is below \(6 + 6 = 12\), so ES stays subadditive.
:::

## Spectral risk measures

A risk measure computed from a loss distribution can be described by the weights it places on each percentile of that distribution.

- **VaR** puts all its weight on the \(X\) percentile and none anywhere else.
- **ES** puts no weight below the \(X\) percentile and equal weight on every percentile above it.

A measure defined by percentile weights \(w(p)\) is coherent if and only if the weights never decrease as the percentile rises, so a worse outcome always receives at least as much weight as a better one.

:::formula Coherence condition for percentile weights
$$w(p_1)\ \ge\ w(p_2)\quad\text{whenever}\quad p_1 > p_2$$
- \(p\): percentile of the loss distribution; a higher \(p\) is a larger loss
:::

VaR breaks the condition. At 99% confidence, \(w(0.99) = 1\) while \(w(0.995) = 0\), so a worse outcome receives less weight. ES meets it because its weights are flat above \(X\).

A **spectral risk measure** uses weights that are non-decreasing in the percentile and shaped by risk aversion, so every spectral risk measure is coherent. One proposed shape is exponential:

:::formula Exponential spectral weights
$$w(p) \propto e^{-(1-p)/\gamma}$$
- \(\gamma\): risk-aversion parameter; a smaller \(\gamma\) concentrates weight on the most extreme losses and represents greater risk aversion
:::

With \(\gamma = 0.05\), the 99th percentile receives \(e^{0.09/0.05} = 6.05\) times the weight of the 90th percentile. With \(\gamma = 0.15\), it receives \(e^{0.09/0.15} = 1.82\) times the weight.

## Practice questions

:::question
Investment X has an expected return of 8% and a standard deviation of 12%. Investment Y has an expected return of 12% and a standard deviation of 25%. The correlation between them is −0.2. What are the expected return and standard deviation of a portfolio with 70% in X and 30% in Y?
- [x] 9.2% and 10.1%
- [ ] 9.2% and 15.9%
- [ ] 10.0% and 10.1%
- [ ] 9.2% and 11.3%
---
Expected return: \(0.7(8\%) + 0.3(12\%) = 9.2\%\).

Variance: \(0.7^2(0.12)^2 + 0.3^2(0.25)^2 + 2(-0.2)(0.7)(0.3)(0.12)(0.25) = 0.007056 + 0.005625 - 0.002520 = 0.010161\), so \(\sigma_P = 10.08\%\).

15.9% is the weighted average of the standard deviations, correct only when \(\rho = 1\). 11.3% leaves out the covariance term. 10.0% is the unweighted average of the two returns.
:::

:::question
An investor judges portfolios only by mean and standard deviation. Which portfolio cannot lie on the efficient frontier?

| Portfolio | Expected return | Standard deviation |
|---|---|---|
| P | 5.0% | 8.0% |
| Q | 6.0% | 7.5% |
| R | 7.0% | 10.0% |
| S | 9.0% | 15.0% |

- [x] P
- [ ] Q
- [ ] R
- [ ] S
---
Q has a higher expected return (6.0% against 5.0%) and a lower standard deviation (7.5% against 8.0%) than P, so Q dominates P. No listed portfolio has both a higher return and a lower standard deviation than Q, R, or S.
:::

:::question
A trading desk's 10-day profit is normally distributed with a mean of USD 1.5 million and a standard deviation of USD 8 million. What is the 10-day 99% VaR?
- [ ] USD 11.7 million
- [x] USD 17.1 million
- [ ] USD 18.6 million
- [ ] USD 20.1 million
---
\(\VaR = -\mu + z_{0.99}\,\sigma = -1.5 + 2.326 \times 8 = 17.108\), or USD 17.1 million.

USD 18.6 million ignores the expected profit. USD 20.1 million adds the expected profit as if it were a loss. USD 11.7 million uses the 95% value 1.645 in place of 2.326.
:::

:::question
Daily changes in a portfolio's value are independent, with a mean of USD 0.1 million and a standard deviation of USD 1.5 million. What are the mean and standard deviation of the change over 25 trading days?
- [x] Mean USD 2.5 million; standard deviation USD 7.5 million
- [ ] Mean USD 0.5 million; standard deviation USD 7.5 million
- [ ] Mean USD 2.5 million; standard deviation USD 37.5 million
- [ ] Mean USD 0.5 million; standard deviation USD 37.5 million
---
The mean scales with \(T\): \(0.1 \times 25 = 2.5\). Variances add across independent days, so the standard deviation scales with \(\sqrt{T}\): \(1.5 \times \sqrt{25} = 7.5\).

The other choices scale the mean by \(\sqrt{T}\), the standard deviation by \(T\), or both.
:::

:::question
A position has the one-year loss distribution below (USD millions). What are its 95% VaR and 95% expected shortfall?

| Loss | Probability |
|---|---|
| 0 | 90.0% |
| 5 | 6.0% |
| 12 | 3.2% |
| 30 | 0.8% |

- [ ] VaR 12; ES 15.6
- [x] VaR 5; ES 13.48
- [ ] VaR 5; ES 15.6
- [ ] VaR 5; ES 9.24
---
Cumulative probabilities are 90.0% (loss 0), 96.0% (loss 5), 99.2% (loss 12), and 100% (loss 30). 95% falls in the band that belongs to a loss of 5, so VaR is 5.

The 5% tail holds 0.8% at 30, 3.2% at 12, and the remaining 1.0% at 5:
\(\ES = (0.008 \times 30 + 0.032 \times 12 + 0.010 \times 5)/0.05 = 0.674/0.05 = 13.48\).

15.6 averages only the 4% of probability at 12 and 30 and omits the 1% at 5 needed to fill a 5% tail. 9.24 averages every positive loss, which is a 10% tail.

:::question
One-year profit is equally likely to take any value from a loss of 30 to a gain of 50 (USD millions). What are the 90% VaR and 90% expected shortfall?
- [x] VaR 22; ES 26
- [ ] VaR 27; ES 28.5
- [ ] VaR 22; ES 15
- [ ] VaR 26; ES 28
---
The range is 80 wide, so the worst 10% of outcomes span \(0.10 \times 80 = 8\): losses from 22 to 30. VaR is 22, and ES is the midpoint of that band, 26.

27 and 28.5 take 10% of only the loss side of the range (30 wide). 15 is the average of all losses, not of the tail. 26 and 28 are the 95% figures.
:::

:::question
A risk measure falls by exactly USD 5 million when USD 5 million of cash is added to the portfolio. Which property of a coherent risk measure does this describe?
- [ ] Monotonicity
- [x] Translation invariance
- [ ] Homogeneity
- [ ] Subadditivity
---
Translation invariance states \(\rho(P + K) = \rho(P) - K\): cash absorbs losses one-for-one, so it substitutes for capital. Monotonicity compares portfolios that do worse in every state, homogeneity concerns scaling all positions, and subadditivity concerns merging portfolios.
:::

:::question
Two independent bonds each lose USD 100 with probability 4% and nothing otherwise. What is the 95% VaR of a portfolio holding both, and what does the result show?
- [x] USD 100; VaR is not subadditive
- [ ] USD 0; VaR is subadditive in this case
- [ ] USD 200; VaR is not homogeneous
- [ ] USD 100; VaR is not monotonic
---
Alone, each bond has a 4% chance of a loss, below 5%, so each stand-alone 95% VaR is 0.

Together: no loss with probability \(0.96^2 = 92.16\%\), a loss of 100 with probability \(2 \times 0.04 \times 0.96 = 7.68\%\) (cumulative 99.84%), and a loss of 200 with probability 0.16%. 95% falls in the band for a loss of 100, so portfolio VaR is 100, larger than \(0 + 0\). Combining the positions raised VaR, which violates subadditivity.
:::

:::question
A risk measure is defined as a weighted average of the percentiles of the loss distribution. Which weighting always produces a coherent measure?
- [ ] All weight on the 99th percentile
- [ ] Weights that decline as the percentile rises above 95%
- [x] Weights that never decrease as the percentile rises
- [ ] Equal weights on all percentiles below the 95th and zero weight above
---
A percentile-weighted measure is coherent if and only if \(w(p_1) \ge w(p_2)\) whenever \(p_1 > p_2\). All weight on the 99th percentile is 99% VaR, which gives zero weight to worse percentiles. The other two schemes also give less weight to larger losses.
:::

:::question
Daily returns on an equity portfolio are more peaked and fatter-tailed than a normal distribution with the same standard deviation. A 99.9% one-day VaR computed under a normal assumption is most likely:
- [x] Too low, because extreme losses are more probable than the normal distribution implies
- [ ] Too high, because small moves are more common than the normal distribution implies
- [ ] Accurate, because the standard deviation is estimated correctly
- [ ] Too high, because moderate moves are less common than the normal distribution implies
---
A 99.9% VaR depends on the far tail. Fat tails place more probability there than a normal distribution with the same standard deviation, so the true 99.9% percentile loss is larger than the normal estimate. How often small and moderate moves occur does not determine the 0.1% tail.
:::

:::question
Daily profit is normally distributed with mean zero and standard deviation USD 4 million. What is the 97.5% expected shortfall?
- [ ] USD 7.84 million
- [ ] USD 9.30 million
- [x] USD 9.35 million
- [ ] USD 10.66 million
---
\(z_{0.975} = 1.960\) and \(\phi(1.960) = e^{-1.960^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi} = 0.05845\).

\(\ES = 4 \times 0.05845/0.025 = 9.35\), or USD 9.35 million.

USD 7.84 million is 97.5% VaR (\(1.960 \times 4\)). USD 9.30 million is 99% VaR (\(2.326 \times 4\)). USD 10.66 million is 99% ES (\(2.665 \times 4\)).
:::

:::question
The risk-free rate is 2%. The market portfolio has an expected return of 8% and a standard deviation of 16%. An investor on the line through the risk-free asset and the market portfolio targets an expected return of 11%. What standard deviation does the investor bear, and how is the position financed?
- [x] 24.0%, borrowing an amount equal to 50% of wealth at the risk-free rate
- [ ] 24.0%, lending 50% of wealth at the risk-free rate
- [ ] 16.0%, holding only the market portfolio
- [ ] 19.6%, borrowing an amount equal to 50% of wealth at the risk-free rate
---
\(w_M = (11\% - 2\%)/(8\% - 2\%) = 1.5\), so the investor holds 150% of wealth in the market portfolio and borrows the extra 50% at 2%. Standard deviation \(= 1.5 \times 16\% = 24.0\%\).

Lending would put the investor between the risk-free asset and the market portfolio, with an expected return below 8%. 19.6% scales the standard deviation by \(\sqrt{1.5}\), but leverage scales it by 1.5.
:::
