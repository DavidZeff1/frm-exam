## Risk, risk taking, and risk management

**Risk** is the possibility that outcomes turn out worse than expected. **Risk taking** is accepting that possibility in exchange for a reward: a bank lends to earn an interest margin, and a farmer plants to sell a crop. **Risk management** decides which risks to take and in what size. It asks two questions of every exposure: is the risk commensurate with the reward, and could the same reward be earned with less risk?

Risk management does not aim to remove risk. A firm that sheds risks that destroy value can take on more of the risks that create value, which is how better risk management lets farmers grow more food, metals producers produce more metal, and banks lend more. Most risk management disasters come from getting a basic element wrong (ignoring a type of risk, misreading how risks connect, or skipping steps of the process), not from the failure of a sophisticated technique.

## A typology of risks

A **risk typology** sorts risks into classes. Firms use it to find the risk factors inside each class, to map processes so no risk falls between functions, and to make staff accountable for specific risks. Bank risk functions are often organized along the same lines. A typology has to change with practice: a banking typology written in the early 1990s might have left out rogue trading or the whole operational risk class, and cyber risk and data privacy risk have since moved up risk managers' lists.

Banks treat market and credit risk as part of their business because taking more of them earns more reward, so they pursue them within limits. Operational risk earns no extra reward, so banks avoid it where they can.

| Class | Definition | Main forms |
|---|---|---|
| Market risk | Loss from changes in market prices and rates | Equity, interest rate, currency, and commodity risk; general and specific risk; basis risk |
| Credit risk | Loss from a party failing to meet its financial obligations | Default (bankruptcy), downgrade, counterparty, and settlement (Herstatt) risk |
| Liquidity risk | Loss from being unable to fund obligations or to trade assets at fair prices | Funding liquidity risk, market (trading) liquidity risk |
| Operational risk | Loss from inadequate or failed processes, people, and systems, or from external events | Legal, rogue trading, cyber, anti-money-laundering failures, external events |
| Business risk | Loss from the ordinary uncertainties of running a firm | Customer demand, pricing, supplier terms, competition, product innovation |
| Strategic risk | Loss from large, long-term decisions about the firm's direction | Major commitments of capital, people, and management reputation |
| Reputation risk | A sudden fall in market standing or brand, with economic consequences | Loss of confidence in soundness or fair dealing; loss of regulators' trust |

### Market risk

Market prices and rates move continuously, and price volatility is what generates market risk. For financial institutions the main forms are equity, interest rate, currency, and commodity price risk. In every market there are two drivers:

- **General market risk**: an entire asset class falls in value, pulling down individual assets and portfolios.
- **Specific market risk**: an individual asset falls by more than its asset class.

Relationships between positions both reduce and create market risk. Diversification across a large equity portfolio lowers risk. A portfolio built to track a benchmark can fail to track it. A hedge can offset the exposure it protects only partly, which is **basis risk**. An airline that hedges jet fuel with crude oil futures, relying on the historical link between the two prices, loses protection if that link breaks, and can end up worse off than with no hedge at all.

### Credit risk

**Credit risk** is the risk that one party fails to meet its financial obligations to another. Its forms:

- **Default (bankruptcy) risk**: a debtor does not pay interest or principal.
- **Downgrade risk**: an obligor's credit rating is cut, which immediately lowers the value of securities linked to its credit.
- **Counterparty risk**: a counterparty to a trade fails to perform. It includes **settlement risk**, also called **Herstatt risk** after Bankhaus Herstatt. German regulators closed Herstatt in 1974 after its counterparties had paid it Deutsche marks but before it paid them the US dollars it owed in return.

Three quantities drive credit risk: the probability that the obligor defaults, the exposure at the time of default, and the amount recovered. A lender changes them through the quality of its borrowers, the structure of the instrument (whether it is collateralized and with what, the lender's priority in bankruptcy, and covenants restricting the borrower), and limits on exposure. A loan's exposure is set by its terms. A derivative's exposure can start at zero and grow quickly when market moves make the contract valuable to one side.

A loan portfolio's credit risk also depends on how its loans relate to each other. The portfolio is riskier when:

- It holds a few large loans rather than many small ones (concentration).
- Borrowers' default probabilities move together, for example because they share an industry or region.
- Exposure, default probability, and loss given default move together, for example when recoveries fall as defaults rise.

### Liquidity risk

**Funding liquidity risk** is the risk that a firm cannot obtain enough cash or liquid assets to meet its obligations. Small, fast-growing firms face it when bills fall due before revenue arrives. Banks carry it by design: they take short-term deposits and make longer-term loans, a maturity mismatch managed through **asset-liability management (ALM)** techniques such as gap analysis and duration analysis. Many banks that failed in 2007–2009 had built up large maturity mismatches and depended on how wholesale funding markets viewed their creditworthiness.

**Market liquidity risk**, also called trading liquidity risk, is the risk of loss when a market seizes up and a seller must accept an abnormally low price or cannot sell at any price. Liquidity measures taken in normal markets, such as trading volume and the bid-ask spread, say little about whether a market will stay liquid in a crisis. For a bank that depends on fragile wholesale funding, market liquidity risk can become funding liquidity risk overnight.

### Operational risk

Bank regulators define **operational risk** as the risk of loss resulting from inadequate or failed internal processes, people, and systems, or from external events. The definition includes legal risk and excludes business, strategic, and reputation risk. It covers anti-money-laundering failures, cyberattacks, terrorist attacks, and rogue trading; the rogue trading losses of the 1990s helped persuade regulators to require capital for operational risk. Outside finance, industrial accidents and governance scandals, such as the collapse of Enron in 2001, belong to the same class, and non-financial firms manage much of it through insurance.

Measuring operational risk is still unsettled. From the late 1990s, banks built loss databases, scorecards, key risk indicators, and scenario analysis to calculate operational risk capital. In 2016 the Basel Committee changed direction: banks should keep using those tools to understand the risk, but capital is set by a simpler standardized approach based on a weighted measure of bank size, with a multiplier that reflects the bank's history of large operational losses.

### Business and strategic risk

**Business risk** covers the ordinary uncertainties of running a firm: customer demand, pricing, supplier negotiations, competition, and product innovation. **Strategic risk** comes from large, long-term decisions about the firm's direction that commit capital, people, and management reputation, for example whether a bank should build its own financial technology services, buy FinTech firms, or partner with them. General management owns both risks. Risk managers still have three roles:

1. Management should set a risk appetite that covers significant business and strategic decisions, not only financial risks. A firm can be conservative in credit risk and entrepreneurial in business risk, but management should state the reason for the difference.
2. Risk teams bring quantitative skills. Credit experts often help manage supply chain risk, and macroeconomic scenario analysis can improve strategic decisions.
3. Business decisions create exposures in other risk classes. Financing a power station may be impossible without a plan for managing energy price risk, and growing a lending business raises credit exposure and may require lowering credit standards. Risk managers therefore need to be involved at the start of business planning.

### Reputation risk

**Reputation risk** is the danger of a sudden fall in a firm's market standing or brand with economic consequences, such as the loss of customers or counterparties. It usually follows a failure in another area. A large credit loss can start rumors about a bank's soundness, and rumors alone can bring a bank down because depositors and investors withdraw in the expectation that others will. A firm that misrepresents a product's risks loses its reputation for fair dealing and the customers that came with it. Standing with regulators matters to financial firms because regulators hold informal as well as formal power: a bank that loses a regulator's trust faces more examinations and restrictions on its activities.

### How risks interact

Risk flows from one class to another. In the 2007–2009 crisis, risk flowed from credit risk to liquidity risk to market risk. Inside one firm, a trader's keying error (operational risk) can create a large unintended position (market risk) that damages the firm's standing (reputation risk). New risks usually grow out of changes in practice: rogue trading risk grew with derivatives and proprietary trading, bank liquidity risk grew with changes in funding strategies and leverage, legal risk grew with class-action claims after the crisis, and cyber risk grew with the digital revolution.

## The risk management process

The process has four steps:

1. **Identify** the risk.
2. **Analyze** and measure it.
3. **Assess** the effects of a risk event.
4. **Manage** the risk.

Which kind of risk an exposure is shapes the response as much as its size. Manufacturers accept the operational risks of running complex factories but tend to avoid or transfer large market and credit risks. Investors react badly to losses from risks they consider foreign to a firm, such as a speculative derivatives loss at a non-financial company.

### Four responses to a risk

| Response | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Avoid** | Stop the activity, or pursue it another way | Not selling into certain markets, or not moving production offshore, to avoid political or foreign exchange risk |
| **Retain** | Keep the risk, within the firm's risk appetite | Allocating risk capital, self-insuring, or using a captive insurer |
| **Mitigate** | Reduce exposure, frequency, or severity | Better operational infrastructure (fewer incidents); hedging currency exposure; taking collateral (smaller loss if the borrower defaults) |
| **Transfer** | Pass the risk to a third party | Buying insurance, derivatives, or structured products |

:::error Classifying a captive insurer as risk transfer
A captive insurance company is owned by the firm it insures, so fire or liability losses paid by the captive remain losses of the group. The risk is retained. Transfer requires a third party, such as an outside insurer or a derivatives counterparty, to bear the loss.
:::

### Identifying risks

Common ways to identify and triage risks:

- **Brainstorming** with business leaders, audit staff, and other professionals: what could go wrong, why, how badly, what it would trigger, and who is accountable.
- **Structured interviews, questionnaires, and surveys** that carry the inquiry across the firm or industry, using open-ended questions.
- **Industry resources**: checklists, professional and regulatory standards, industry surveys, and expert opinion.
- **Loss data analysis**: internal and external loss records used to estimate each risk's frequency and severity and to link losses to risk factors.
- **Basic triage**: ranking each risk by frequency and severity even where exact measurement is impossible.
- **What-if analysis** of worst-case scenarios.
- **Front-line observation** of how work is actually done, with front-line staff included in gathering information.
- **Following the trail** through key processes to find gaps, and tracing the worst outcomes backward through them.

## Known and unknown risks

Some risks can be measured well and others not at all. Donald Rumsfeld's 2002 remark separates **known knowns**, **known unknowns**, and **unknown unknowns**: people focus on the risks they have data for and neglect potentially larger ones that are unknown or poorly understood.

Two economists drew the same kind of line in 1921:

| Economist | Measurable | Not measurable |
|---|---|---|
| Frank Knight | **Risk** ("measurable risk"): the outcome is unknown, but its probabilities can be estimated | **Uncertainty** ("true uncertainty"): the information needed to assign probabilities is unavailable; now called **Knightian uncertainty** |
| John Maynard Keynes | Risk that can be calculated | **Irreducible uncertainty**: calculating it would require assumptions about the future with no basis in probability theory |

Knightian uncertainty can be very large and still be managed. No one can estimate the probability of nuclear war, but multilateral disarmament would remove the risk; acting on it requires agreement that the threat is plausible and severe. The boundary between uncertainty and measurable risk also moves. Before 1950 the health threat from smoking was uncertain, and by the mid-1970s research had turned it into a quantified risk: about one in two long-term smokers die from the habit.

Risk managers are responsible for every risk, not only the measurable ones. They should move poorly understood risks toward measurement where they can, and they must never treat a risk that cannot be measured as if it were a known quantity.

Many risks are unseen rather than unknown. Alex Brazier of the Bank of England described them in 2018:

| Type | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Moonwalking bear** | A risk in plain view that goes unnoticed because attention is elsewhere, named after a video in which viewers counting basketball passes miss a person in a bear suit. Compressed debt market yields show that risk is priced too cheaply, yet investors keep buying. |
| **Underwater iceberg** | A risk that is hard to spot beforehand and obvious afterward because it rests on a fundamental weakness, such as the build-up of leverage at financial firms before 2007–2009. |
| **Elephant in the room** | A risk that everyone has spotted but that no one will acknowledge publicly. |

## Expected, unexpected, and tail loss

### Expected loss

**Expected loss (EL)** is the average loss that a position or portfolio is expected to produce. It depends on the probability of a loss event, the firm's exposure to that event, and the severity of the loss when it happens. For a loan, these are the **probability of default (PD)**, the **exposure at default (EAD)**, and the **loss given default (LGD)**, which is the fraction of the exposure lost after recoveries.

:::formula Expected loss on a credit exposure
$$\text{EL} = \text{EAD} \times \text{LGD} \times \text{PD}$$
- EAD: amount owed when the borrower defaults
- LGD: fraction of EAD lost, equal to one minus the recovery rate
- PD: probability of default over the horizon
:::

When EL can be estimated with confidence, it behaves like a predictable cost of doing business rather than a risk. The lender covers it by adding a margin to the price, and the risk manager's job becomes measuring EL and making sure the portfolio stays predictable.

:::example Expected loss priced into a loan
A bank lends USD 10 million to a borrower with a one-year PD of 2%. It expects to recover 60% of the exposure if the borrower defaults.

1. LGD \(= 1 - 0.60 = 0.40\).
2. EL \(= 10{,}000{,}000 \times 0.40 \times 0.02 = 80{,}000\) per year.
3. As a share of the loan, \(80{,}000/10{,}000{,}000 = 0.80\%\). The loan rate needs a margin of 80 basis points to cover expected losses, before funding and operating costs.
:::

### Unexpected loss

Actual losses depart from EL. A fraud or an unlucky run of defaults makes some years worse than average, and the size of that departure is the **unexpected loss**. In a credit portfolio it depends on granularity and diversification: with many small loans, no single default matters much, and when borrowers are unrelated, defaults rarely cluster. EL and unexpected loss both change as the economy and the portfolio's composition change.

:::example Same expected loss, different unexpected loss
Portfolio 1 is a single USD 10 million loan. Portfolio 2 is 1,000 independent loans of USD 10,000 each. Every loan has a PD of 2% and an LGD of 40%.

1. EL is the same for both: \(10{,}000{,}000 \times 0.40 \times 0.02 = 80{,}000\).
2. Portfolio 1 loses either nothing (probability 98%) or USD 4 million (probability 2%). The standard deviation of its loss is \(4{,}000{,}000 \times \sqrt{0.02 \times 0.98} = 560{,}000\).
3. In Portfolio 2, the number of defaults has variance \(1{,}000 \times 0.02 \times 0.98 = 19.6\), and each default costs USD 4,000. The standard deviation of its loss is \(\sqrt{19.6} \times 4{,}000 = 17{,}709\).

For the same EL, the concentrated portfolio's loss is about 32 times as variable. If the 1,000 borrowers' defaults were correlated, Portfolio 2's variability would rise toward Portfolio 1's.
:::

### Extreme losses and risky relationships

Some portfolios combine long runs of good years with short runs of very bad years. Their average loss conceals the risk: the lender sees years of low losses, becomes complacent, and takes a large loss when the cycle turns. These portfolios need risk capital for large unexpected losses on top of the EL built into prices.

Commercial real estate (CRE) lending follows this cycle:

1. Demand for property rises with the economy. Supply is **inelastic** because buildings take years to construct, so prices rise.
2. Rising prices attract investors and lenders, who relax loan-to-value limits and other safeguards to win market share.
3. New supply arrives as the economy weakens. Prices fall, banks withdraw credit, and developers run short of cash.
4. Property values, which are the lenders' collateral, fall at the same time as developers' default probabilities rise, and forced sales push prices down further.

**Wrong-way risk** is the combination in which the probability of default rises together with the loss the lender would suffer. CRE shows it through default probabilities rising while collateral values, and therefore recoveries, fall. In derivatives it appears when a contract becomes more valuable to a firm at the same time as the counterparty becomes more likely to default. Breaking credit risk into PD, LGD, and EAD is what lets analysts see how these factors move together.

### VaR and expected shortfall

In January 1990, Dennis Weatherstone, the new chief executive of J.P. Morgan, asked for a report of the bank's total risk on his desk at 4:15 p.m. every day. That request drove the development of **value-at-risk (VaR)**. Philippe Jorion defines VaR as the worst expected loss over a given horizon under normal market conditions at a given level of confidence.

- A one-day 99% VaR of USD 5 million means a 1% probability of losing more than USD 5 million in one day under normal market conditions.
- A monthly 95% VaR of 4% of fund value means a 5% probability that the fund loses more than 4% in a month.

For a given loss distribution, lowering the confidence level (from 99% to 95%, say) lowers VaR, and a fatter loss tail raises it. VaR ignores the size of losses beyond the threshold. **Expected shortfall (ES)**, also called conditional VaR (CVaR), measures them: it is the average of the losses larger than VaR at the chosen tail probability. [Book 4, Chapter 1](#/b4-01) develops both measures.

## Breaking risk into risk factors

Each primary risk factor has its own drivers. A company's PD depends on its financial ratios, industry sector, management quality, and similar factors, and management quality in turn depends on sub-factors such as the managers' years of experience. Understanding how factors drive losses, how they interact with each other and the business environment, and how they behave under stress is a core risk management task.

How granular the analysis can be is limited by analytical resources and by loss data, which may be scarce, poor in quality, or short on descriptive detail. Regulation can force granularity: to prepare for Basel II, banks spent millions re-engineering their rating systems to record PD, LGD, and exposure as separate factors.

Data science extends what is possible. Insurers combine public, social, credit rating, and unstructured data to assess risk for a "segment of one." **Unsupervised machine learning** can surface unknown unknowns by finding clusters and correlations without being told in advance where to look.

## Structural change

Rare, extreme events occur even in a structurally stable system, and a long enough data history should reveal them. Where data are scarce, **extreme value theory (EVT)**, a branch of statistics focused on tails, helps estimate their size.

When the structure of a system changes, history stops being a reliable guide. Large losses become more frequent or larger, risk factors start to move in lockstep, and entirely new sources of loss appear. More historical data does not help, and a "once-in-100-years" loss can recur every decade until the structural problem is fixed. EL and unexpected loss change as well as the tail. Financial markets change structure continually because social behavior, industry trends, regulation, and product innovation keep changing.

Before the 2007–2009 crisis, interest-only mortgages and mortgages with below-market initial rates grew from a small fraction of US originations to a substantial share, while the proportion of subprime loans (to borrowers with blemished credit histories) also rose. Loss data from earlier mortgage lending could not show how this new mix would perform.

## Human agency and conflicts of interest

People in financial markets react to change and to controls. Traders try to anticipate how other traders will react to a reform, and a regulator who helped draft a rule can join a consultancy that advises firms on how to get around it. Inside a firm, the people who understand best how risk is generated are best placed to game the controls and have the least incentive to reveal potential losses.

Many financial firms respond with **three lines of defense**:

| Line | Who | Role |
|---|---|---|
| First | Business lines | Generate, own, and manage risk |
| Second | Risk management function | Specialist, day-to-day oversight |
| Third | Internal audit | Periodic independent oversight and assurance |

The lines are not watertight. Controls develop loopholes and fall behind product innovation. In a number of bank rogue trading cases, the trader had previously worked in the middle or back office and knew the gaps in the control system. Traders and business heads sometimes deliberately undermine the credibility of risk management systems.

## Aggregating risk

Senior managers need to know which businesses are riskiest and when the firm's total risk is approaching intolerable levels, and that requires combining risks.

- **Notional amounts** were the old yardstick for market risk (for example, USD 10 million of a large-cap stock). Notional ignores volatility, so it cannot compare a Treasury desk with a commodity desk, and it overstates the risk of derivatives portfolios designed so that positions offset each other.
- **The Greeks**, such as delta (the sensitivity of an option's value to the underlying price) and theta (the change in option value as expiration approaches), are essential on an options desk but cannot be added across desks: one unit of delta in foreign exchange does not carry the same risk as one unit of delta in commodities.
- **VaR** produced one figure across positions and became the standard aggregate measure before 2007–2009. It rests on simplifying assumptions, there are at least three main ways to calculate it, and it was stretched to long horizons, entire industries, and many risk types. The crisis exposed these limits. Regulators have since made VaR calculations more consistent and given more weight to expected shortfall and worst-case scenario analysis.

Any aggregate measure misses some dimensions of risk, so firms supplement it:

| Tool | Starting point | What it produces |
|---|---|---|
| **Scenario analysis and stress testing** | A plausible worst-case scenario, often unfolding in stages, designed by the firm or supplied by a regulator | The loss the scenario would cause given the firm's exposures and ability to react; severity, not probability |
| **Reverse stress testing** | A target loss level the firm could not tolerate | The scenarios and risk factors that would produce that loss, and the changes to activities that would prevent it |

## Balancing risk and reward

### Economic capital

**Economic capital**, or risk capital, is the capital a firm needs based on its own assessment of its economic risks. It is held to absorb unexpected losses; expected losses are covered through pricing. **Regulatory capital** is calculated under regulators' rules and methods. The two figures are sometimes close and often quite different.

Economic capital puts dissimilar businesses on a common footing. Business A has steady losses every year but few surprises. Business B has almost no losses in normal years and very large losses at the end of each business cycle. In the benign part of the cycle B looks more profitable, and a firm that ignores its end-of-cycle risk may cut B's prices to build volume, which enlarges the losses when the cycle turns. Banking industries around the world have behaved this way repeatedly, amplifying booms and busts.

### RAROC

**Risk-adjusted return on capital (RAROC)** divides reward by risk, measuring reward as after-tax expected return net of expected losses and risk as economic capital.

:::formula RAROC
$$\text{RAROC}=\frac{\text{After-tax net risk-adjusted expected return}}{\text{Economic capital}}$$
- Net risk-adjusted expected return: expected revenues minus costs minus expected losses
- Economic capital: capital required to absorb unexpected losses
:::

An activity adds value for shareholders when its RAROC exceeds the **hurdle rate**, the cost of equity capital that shareholders require as fair compensation for risk.

:::example Comparing two businesses with RAROC
The tax rate is 25% and the hurdle rate is 12%. Figures are USD millions per year.

| | Business A | Business B |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue minus operating costs | 30 | 26 |
| Expected loss | 12 | 2 |
| Risk-adjusted return before tax | 18 | 24 |
| Risk-adjusted return after tax | 13.5 | 18.0 |
| Economic capital | 50 | 200 |
| RAROC | 27.0% | 9.0% |

1. Business A: \(13.5/50 = 27.0\%\), above the 12% hurdle, so it adds value.
2. Business B: \(18.0/200 = 9.0\%\), below the hurdle, so it destroys value. Its return is larger, but its end-of-cycle losses require four times as much capital.
:::

Four common uses of RAROC:

- **Business comparison**: ranking business lines that need different amounts of economic capital.
- **Investment analysis**: projected RAROC for a proposed product, or historical RAROC compared with the hurdle rate for an existing business.
- **Pricing strategy**: finding customer segments priced too low to earn a risk-adjusted profit, and segments where prices could be cut to gain market share.
- **Risk management cost-benefit analysis**: comparing the cost of risk management, such as buying insurance, with its benefit to the firm.

RAROC is only as reliable as the risk calculations beneath it, and business managers often dispute the numbers, sometimes out of self-interest. Risk metrics also differ in what they capture. A figure built as probability × exposure × severity attempts an absolute measure of risk. A report that tracks only one component can mislead: a bank that loosens credit standards while losing market share may show falling loan volume without any fall in credit risk. **Key risk indicators (KRIs)** are quantitative measures used as proxies for potential exposures, such as staff turnover as an indicator of operational risk. The link between a KRI and the risk it tracks rests on judgment, so a moving KRI is not the risk itself.

## Enterprise risk management

Business divisions often manage risk in **silos**, each handling its own exposures without considering the others. **Enterprise risk management (ERM)** builds a firm-wide picture across risk types and business lines, using tools such as a clear risk appetite statement and firm-wide risk committees. [Book 1, Chapter 8](#/b1-08) covers ERM in detail.

Early ERM programs put too much weight on expressing total risk as one number, such as economic capital or VaR. The 2007–2009 crisis showed that risk cannot be reduced to a single number because:

- It is multidimensional and needs several methods, applied from different angles.
- It develops over time and crosses risk types, so even a wide view taken at one moment can miss it.
- It needs expert judgment combined with statistical analysis.

The analysis that saves a firm may come from a worst-case scenario, from noticing a structural change in a market, from seeing that behavior across the industry could set off a crisis, or from a market-based credit indicator that flags trouble at a counterparty early enough to act. ERM also covers what happens after the warning: the governance and risk culture that turn information into action, and a tested process for changing course when a growth strategy turns out to carry risks nobody understood. Modern ERM therefore uses the full range of risk tools, looks across several time horizons, joins up businesses and risk types, takes an industry-wide perspective, and pays attention to people, how they communicate, and "the way we do things."

Digitization is changing risk functions more slowly than customer-facing operations, according to a 2017 survey by McKinsey and the Institute of International Finance. The changes under way are wider data sources analyzed with advanced analytics (for example in credit and operational risk), faster automated decisions such as automated corporate credit scoring, and automated workflows in place of paper document reviews. The obstacles are legacy infrastructure, limited data, and a shortage of digital skills, which puts data scientists in high demand.

## Practice questions

:::question
A bank has a USD 25 million exposure to a borrower with a probability of default of 1.5% and an expected recovery rate of 40%. What is the expected loss?
- [ ] USD 150,000
- [x] USD 225,000
- [ ] USD 375,000
- [ ] USD 15,000,000
---
LGD \(= 1 - 0.40 = 0.60\), so EL \(= 25{,}000{,}000 \times 0.60 \times 0.015 = 225{,}000\).

USD 150,000 uses the recovery rate (40%) in place of LGD. USD 375,000 omits LGD, which assumes the whole exposure is lost. USD 15 million is EAD × LGD, the loss if default occurs, without weighting by the probability of default.
:::

:::question
Which situation is an example of funding liquidity risk?
- [x] A bank that finances long-term loans with overnight wholesale borrowing cannot roll over that borrowing
- [ ] A dealer can sell its corporate bonds only at a deep discount because buyers have left the market
- [ ] A bank pays Deutsche marks to a counterparty that is closed before paying the dollars it owes
- [ ] A bond portfolio falls in value when interest rates rise
---
Funding liquidity risk is the inability to obtain cash to meet obligations, and a maturity mismatch financed by short-term wholesale borrowing is its classic source. Selling only at a deep discount is market (trading) liquidity risk. Paying one leg of a currency trade without receiving the other is settlement (Herstatt) risk, a form of credit risk. A price fall caused by rising rates is interest rate risk, a form of market risk.
:::

:::question
An airline hedges its jet fuel costs with crude oil futures because the two prices have historically moved together. Jet fuel prices then rise sharply while crude oil prices stay flat. Which risk has the airline experienced?
- [ ] Specific market risk
- [x] Basis risk
- [ ] Counterparty risk
- [ ] Market liquidity risk
---
Basis risk is the risk that a hedge moves differently from the exposure it is meant to offset. The futures produce no gain to offset the higher fuel cost because the historical relationship between the two prices broke down. Specific market risk concerns one asset falling more than its asset class, counterparty risk concerns a party failing to perform, and market liquidity risk concerns the ability to trade at a fair price.
:::

:::question
Under the bank regulatory definition, operational risk includes which of the following?
- [x] Legal risk
- [ ] Strategic risk
- [ ] Reputation risk
- [ ] Business risk
---
Operational risk is the risk of loss from inadequate or failed internal processes, people, and systems, or from external events. The definition explicitly includes legal risk and excludes business, strategic, and reputation risk.

:::question
A manufacturer sets up a captive insurance subsidiary to cover fire damage at its plants. How is this risk response classified?
- [ ] Avoid
- [x] Retain
- [ ] Mitigate
- [ ] Transfer
---
A captive insurer belongs to the corporate group, so the group still bears the fire losses: the risk is retained, like self-insurance or an allocation of risk capital. Transfer would need a third party, such as an outside insurer, to bear the loss. Mitigation would reduce frequency or severity, for example with sprinkler systems. Avoidance would mean not operating the plants.
:::

:::question
Two loan portfolios have the same total exposure and the same expected loss. Which portfolio most likely has the larger unexpected loss?
- [ ] 2,000 small loans to borrowers spread across many industries and regions
- [x] 20 large loans to property developers in one city
- [ ] 2,000 small loans to borrowers in unrelated industries, secured by collateral
- [ ] 500 medium-sized loans to borrowers in unrelated industries
---
Unexpected loss grows with concentration (a few large exposures) and with correlation between borrowers' defaults. Twenty large loans to developers in one city are concentrated and all depend on the same property market, so losses can far exceed their average. Many small loans to unrelated borrowers produce losses that stay close to the expected level.
:::

:::question
A bank's one-day 99% VaR for its trading book is USD 5 million. Which statement is correct?
- [ ] The trading book cannot lose more than USD 5 million in one day
- [x] Under normal market conditions, there is a 1% probability of losing more than USD 5 million in one day
- [ ] The average loss on the worst 1% of days is USD 5 million
- [ ] There is a 99% probability of losing exactly USD 5 million in one day
---
VaR is a loss threshold with a stated probability of being exceeded over a stated horizon: here a 1% chance on any day of a loss above USD 5 million, or about 2.5 days in a 250-day year. VaR is not a maximum loss. The average loss beyond the threshold is expected shortfall, which is at least as large as VaR.
:::

:::question
A lending business has annual revenue of USD 50 million, operating costs of USD 18 million, expected losses of USD 10 million, a 30% tax rate, and economic capital of USD 110 million. Shareholders require a 15% return on equity. What is the business's RAROC, and does it add value?
- [x] 14.0%; it is below the hurdle rate and destroys value
- [ ] 20.4%; it is above the hurdle rate and adds value
- [ ] 20.0%; it is above the hurdle rate and adds value
- [ ] 14.0%; it adds value because it exceeds a 3% risk-free rate
---
After-tax net risk-adjusted return \(= (50 - 18 - 10) \times (1 - 0.30) = 15.4\). RAROC \(= 15.4/110 = 14.0\%\), below the 15% cost of equity, so the business does not earn enough for the capital its risk requires.

20.4% omits expected losses: \((50 - 18) \times 0.70/110\). 20.0% omits tax: \(22/110\). RAROC is compared with the cost of equity capital, not the risk-free rate.
:::

:::question
Which arrangement correctly describes the three lines of defense?
- [x] First: business lines that generate and own risk; second: risk management oversight; third: internal audit
- [ ] First: risk management oversight; second: business lines that generate and own risk; third: internal audit
- [ ] First: business lines that generate and own risk; second: internal audit; third: the board risk committee
- [ ] First: internal audit; second: risk management oversight; third: business lines that generate and own risk
---
The business line that creates the risk is the first line. The risk management function, which specializes in day-to-day oversight, is the second. Internal audit, which provides periodic independent assurance, is the third.
:::

:::question
How does reverse stress testing differ from conventional scenario analysis?
- [ ] It estimates the probability of a scenario rather than its severity
- [x] It starts from a loss level the firm could not tolerate and works back to the scenarios and risk factors that would cause it
- [ ] It uses only historical scenarios, while scenario analysis uses only hypothetical ones
- [ ] It replaces VaR with expected shortfall at a higher confidence level
---
Scenario analysis starts with a plausible severe scenario and calculates the resulting loss. Reverse stress testing starts with the target loss and identifies what would produce it, so the firm can change its activities to prevent that outcome. Both methods focus on severity rather than probability.
:::

:::question
In a commercial real estate downturn, developers become more likely to default at the same time as the properties securing their loans lose value. What is this combination called?
- [x] Wrong-way risk
- [ ] Basis risk
- [ ] Downgrade risk
- [ ] Knightian uncertainty
---
Wrong-way risk occurs when a borrower's or counterparty's probability of default rises together with the loss the lender would suffer. Falling collateral values mean lower recoveries, and so higher LGD, just as PD rises. The same pattern appears in derivatives when a contract gains value for a firm as its counterparty weakens.
:::

:::question
What is economic capital intended to absorb?
- [ ] Expected loss
- [x] Unexpected loss
- [ ] Regulatory capital charges
- [ ] Operating costs
---
Expected loss is a predictable cost covered through pricing. Economic capital is the buffer a firm holds, based on its own assessment of its risks, against losses above that expected level. Regulatory capital is a separate figure set by regulators' rules.
:::

:::question
Which statement matches Frank Knight's distinction between risk and uncertainty?
- [ ] Risk cannot be measured, while uncertainty can be quantified with statistics
- [x] Risk applies when outcome probabilities can be estimated; uncertainty applies when they cannot
- [ ] Risk refers to possible losses and uncertainty refers to possible gains
- [ ] Uncertainty cannot be managed, so risk managers can set it aside
---
Knight's "measurable risk" covers decisions whose outcomes are unknown but whose probabilities can be estimated. "True uncertainty" covers decisions where the information needed to assign probabilities is unavailable. Knightian uncertainty can still be managed, for example by avoiding the activity, and risk managers must not treat it as a known quantity.
