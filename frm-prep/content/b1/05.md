## Models and their limits

Future market moves are uncertain, so risk managers rely on models to measure risk. A good model makes simplifying assumptions that separate the main explanatory variables from background noise without drowning in complexity. Milton Friedman argued that predictive power is the only test of a model: even a very simple model succeeds if it forecasts reasonably well and improves decisions. After 2007–2009 models were blamed for losses, but the failures came from how models were selected, implemented, and over-interpreted. Models remain essential; how they are used matters more.

Market risk has been researched intensively since the 1950s because traded securities generate abundant data, so the two models in this reading, modern portfolio theory and the capital asset pricing model, have been tested empirically in markets around the world.

## Modern portfolio theory

Harry Markowitz's 1952 dissertation founded **modern portfolio theory (MPT)**, which prescribes how investors should construct portfolios, given assumptions about investor behavior and markets. A **rational investor**, one who is risk-averse and seeks to maximize expected utility, judges possible portfolios by the mean and variance of their returns. Given two portfolios with the same expected return, the investor chooses the one with the lower variance.

### Assumptions

- **Perfect capital markets**: no taxes or transaction costs, costless access to all information for every trader, and perfect competition among market participants.
- **Normally distributed returns**, which lets investors' choices be expressed entirely through the mean (performance) and variance (risk). Other things equal, investors prefer a higher mean and a lower variance.

### Diversification and the efficient frontier

Investors reduce variance by **diversifying**: holding assets whose values do not move in lockstep (correlation below 1), so that risks specific to one asset offset those of others. In MPT an asset is not judged on its own. What matters is how its return varies with the return of the whole portfolio, because the portfolio's variance is the weighted sum of each asset's covariance with the portfolio.

:::formula Portfolio variance as a sum of covariances
$$\sigma_P^2=\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i\,\Cov(R_i,R_P)$$
- \(x_i\): weight of asset \(i\) in the portfolio
- \(\Cov(R_i,R_P)\): covariance between asset \(i\)'s return and the portfolio's return
:::

In theory, diversification reduces exposure to individual assets at no cost. Lower risk usually comes with lower expected return, but well-chosen weights maximize the expected return available at each level of risk. Plotting those maximum returns against risk traces the **efficient frontier**:

- A portfolio on the frontier offers the highest expected return for its standard deviation.
- A portfolio below the frontier is suboptimal: portfolios directly above it offer more expected return for the same risk.
- Along the frontier, a higher expected return requires more risk, and lower risk requires accepting a lower expected return.
- The lower edge of the attainable set contains the most inefficient portfolios, with the lowest expected return for each level of risk.

The two-asset calculations that generate the frontier are worked through in [Book 4, Chapter 1](#/b4-01).

### Correlations are unstable

Correlation estimates are a critical input to mean-variance analysis, and correlations between major asset classes rose after the 2007–2009 crisis, even in normal markets. A study by Craig Israelsen of 12-month rolling correlations with US large-cap stocks found:

| Asset class | October 2006 | October 2008 | October 2010 |
|---|---|---|---|
| US bonds | 0.21 | 0.46 | −0.38 |
| Mid-cap US stocks | 0.84 | 0.98 | 0.96 |
| Small-cap US stocks | 0.78 | 0.96 | 0.91 |
| Non-US stocks | 0.63 | 0.92 | 0.90 |
| Emerging market stocks | 0.77 | 0.85 | 0.92 |

Correlations among equity classes were high before the crisis and went higher, which shrinks the benefit of diversifying across them. A common explanation is the growth of basket trading through index funds and exchange-traded funds (ETFs), which trade entire indices at once regardless of analysts' views on individual stocks. The bond correlation moved from 0.21 to 0.46 to −0.38 within four years, which shows how unreliable any single historical estimate can be.

One response is **regime-based allocation**: identify risk regimes and optimize a separate allocation for each. In a high-risk regime (worried markets, high equity volatility, wide credit spreads), the manager shifts toward low-risk assets such as money market funds. In a low-risk regime (low volatility, narrow spreads), the manager shifts toward equities, emerging markets, commodities, and high-yield bonds.

### Problems applying mean-variance optimization

- **Which data to use.** Means, variances, and correlations are estimated from history, but the theory does not say which period to use, and different periods produce very different allocations.
- **Sensitivity to inputs.** Small changes in the inputs produce large changes in the optimal portfolio. Errors in expected returns matter most: they are at least ten times as important as errors in variances and covariances. **Robust portfolio optimization** addresses this by building estimation error directly into the optimization.
- **Non-normal returns.** Evidence across asset classes and countries rejects normality: return distributions have fat tails and are asymmetric. An investor who looks only at mean and variance ignores skewness, and research by Campbell Harvey and Geert Bekaert shows that skewness matters for asset pricing.

## The capital asset pricing model

In the 1960s William Sharpe, John Lintner, and Jan Mossin extended MPT to a model of equilibrium in the whole capital market: the **capital asset pricing model (CAPM)**, which links a risky asset's expected return to its risk.

### Systematic and idiosyncratic risk

The CAPM splits an asset's total risk, the standard deviation of its return, into two parts:

| | Systematic risk | Idiosyncratic risk |
|---|---|---|
| Other names | Market risk, non-diversifiable risk | Unique, specific, or diversifiable risk |
| Source | Movements of the market as a whole | Events specific to the company, such as strikes, adverse regulatory changes, or litigation |
| Removed by diversification | No | Yes |
| Earns a risk premium | Yes | No |
| Measured by | Beta | Variance not explained by the market |

Combining assets in a well-diversified portfolio eliminates idiosyncratic risk and leaves market risk as the only exposure. Because investors can remove idiosyncratic risk for free, the market pays no premium for bearing it: expected returns compensate only for systematic risk.

### CAPM assumptions

Several repeat Markowitz's:

- All information is freely available to every participant and absorbed instantly.
- All participants have the same expectations.
- All participants make decisions based on the mean and variance of returns.
- There are no transaction costs, taxes, or other frictions.
- Investments are perfectly divisible, so any fraction of an asset can be bought.
- Everyone can borrow and lend at a common risk-free rate.
- No individual investor's decisions can change market prices.

Under these assumptions, equilibrium is reached when every investor holds a combination of the risk-free asset and the **market portfolio**, with the proportions set by the investor's risk appetite. Each risky asset's expected return then depends on how much it contributes to the risk of the market portfolio.

### Beta

**Beta** measures an asset's systematic risk: how strongly its return moves with the market's return.

:::formula Beta
$$\beta_i=\frac{\Cov(R_i,R_M)}{\sigma_M^2}=\rho_{iM}\,\frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_M}$$
- \(R_i, R_M\): returns on asset \(i\) and on the market portfolio
- \(\sigma_i, \sigma_M\): their standard deviations
- \(\rho_{iM}\): correlation between the asset's return and the market's return
:::

The market's variance is the weighted sum of every asset's covariance with the market, \(\sigma_M^2=\sum x_i\Cov(R_i,R_M)\). Dividing both sides by \(\sigma_M^2\) gives \(\sum x_i\beta_i = 1\): the value-weighted average beta across the market is 1, so the market portfolio's beta is 1 by construction. Stocks with beta above 1 are **aggressive** because they carry more systematic risk than the market; stocks with beta below 1 are **defensive**. Many US utilities have betas around 0.5.

:::formula Portfolio beta and the split of total variance
$$\beta_P=\sum_{i} w_i\,\beta_i \qquad \sigma_i^2=\underbrace{\beta_i^2\sigma_M^2}_{\text{systematic}}+\underbrace{\sigma_i^2\left(1-\rho_{iM}^2\right)}_{\text{idiosyncratic}}$$
- \(w_i\): weight of asset \(i\) in portfolio \(P\)
- The systematic share of total variance equals \(\rho_{iM}^2\)
:::

:::example Beta, portfolio beta, and the variance split
A stock's return has a standard deviation of 32% and a correlation of 0.55 with the market, whose standard deviation is 20%.

1. Covariance: \(0.55 \times 0.32 \times 0.20 = 0.0352\).
2. Beta: \(0.0352/0.20^2 = 0.88\), which matches \(0.55 \times 0.32/0.20 = 0.88\).
3. Total variance: \(0.32^2 = 0.1024\). Systematic variance: \(0.88^2 \times 0.04 = 0.0310\), which is \(0.55^2 = 30.25\%\) of the total. The other 69.75% is idiosyncratic and can be diversified away.
4. A portfolio holds 40% in a stock with beta 1.3, 35% in a stock with beta 0.8, and 25% in a stock with beta 0.5. Its beta is \(0.40(1.3) + 0.35(0.8) + 0.25(0.5) = 0.52 + 0.28 + 0.125 = 0.925\).
:::

A beta estimated from stock returns reflects the company's financial leverage. An **unlevered beta** removes that effect, which is useful for comparing businesses with different amounts of debt:

:::formula Unlevered beta
$$\beta_U=\frac{\beta_L}{1+(1-t)\,(D/E)}$$
- \(\beta_L\): levered (equity) beta
- \(t\): tax rate; \(D/E\): debt-to-equity ratio
:::

An equity beta of 1.4 with a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.5 and a 25% tax rate gives \(\beta_U = 1.4/(1 + 0.75 \times 0.5) = 1.4/1.375 = 1.02\).

### The CAPM equation

:::formula Capital asset pricing model (security market line)
$$\E(R_i)=r+\beta_i\left[\E(R_M)-r\right]$$
- \(r\): risk-free rate, commonly proxied by the three-month US Treasury bill rate
- \(\E(R_M)-r\): market risk premium, the price of one unit of market risk
- \(\beta_i\): the asset's quantity of market risk
:::

An asset's expected return is the risk-free rate plus a risk premium, and the premium equals the quantity of market risk (beta) times the price per unit of that risk (the market risk premium). In practice a broad index, such as the S&P 500, stands in for the unobservable market portfolio, and the size of the market risk premium is debated.

:::example Expected returns and mispricing under the CAPM
The risk-free rate is 3% and the expected market return is 9%, so the market risk premium is 6%.

1. Portfolio with beta 0.925: \(3\% + 0.925 \times 6\% = 8.55\%\).
2. Stock with beta 1.3: \(3\% + 1.3 \times 6\% = 10.8\%\).
3. An analyst expects the beta-1.3 stock to return 12%, which is 1.2 percentage points above its CAPM required return. The stock plots above the security market line and is undervalued: buying pressure should raise its price until its expected return falls to 10.8%.
:::

A higher total variance does not by itself mean a higher expected return. A very volatile stock can have a low expected return if most of its volatility is idiosyncratic and its beta is low.

## The capital market line and the security market line

Adding a risk-free asset to the efficient frontier produces a straight line from the risk-free rate that touches the frontier at the **tangency portfolio** M. That line is the **capital market line (CML)**. Every portfolio on the CML other than M has a higher expected return than the frontier portfolio with the same standard deviation, so the CML dominates the frontier. The tangency portfolio holds every asset in proportion to its market value: it is the market portfolio.

:::formula Capital market line
$$\E(R_P)=r+\frac{\E(R_M)-r}{\sigma_M}\,\sigma_P$$
- \(\E(R_P), \sigma_P\): expected return and standard deviation of an efficient portfolio
- \((\E(R_M)-r)/\sigma_M\): the slope, the market's reward per unit of total risk
:::

The **two-fund separation theorem** follows: every investor holds just two funds, the risk-free asset and the market portfolio, in proportions set by risk tolerance. Investors with little tolerance for risk put most of their money in the risk-free asset, while those seeking more risk put more into the market portfolio, borrowing if necessary.

| | Capital market line (CML) | Security market line (SML) |
|---|---|---|
| Applies to | Efficient portfolios only: combinations of the risk-free asset and the market portfolio | Every asset and portfolio, efficient or not |
| Risk measure | Standard deviation (total risk) | Beta (systematic risk) |
| Equation | \(\E(R_P)=r+\dfrac{\E(R_M)-r}{\sigma_M}\sigma_P\) | \(\E(R_i)=r+\beta_i[\E(R_M)-r]\) |
| Slope | The market's Sharpe ratio | The market risk premium |
| Points off the line | Inefficient portfolios lie below it | Mispriced assets: above the line is undervalued, below is overvalued |

## Estimating beta

In practice beta is estimated by regressing a security's excess returns on the market's excess returns. This regression is the **market model**:

:::formula Market model regression
$$R_{it}-r_t=a_i+b_i\left(R_{Mt}-r_t\right)+e_{it} \qquad \hat b_i=\frac{\sum_t (x_t-\bar x)(y_t-\bar y)}{\sum_t (x_t-\bar x)^2}$$
- \(y_t = R_{it}-r_t\): the security's excess return in period \(t\); \(x_t = R_{Mt}-r_t\): the market's excess return
- \(a_i\): intercept; \(b_i\): slope, the estimate of \(\beta_i\); \(e_{it}\): residual, uncorrelated with the market
:::

The market model describes realized returns; the CAPM describes expected returns. Subtracting the risk-free rate from both returns shifts the intercept but leaves the slope unchanged. An estimated beta is only an estimate of the true beta: sampling error means the true value lies within a confidence interval around it.

:::example Estimating beta from five months of returns
Monthly excess returns, in percent:

| Month | Market | Stock |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2 | 3 |
| 2 | −1 | −2 |
| 3 | 3 | 5 |
| 4 | −2 | −1 |
| 5 | 3 | 5 |

1. Means: market \(5/5 = 1\), stock \(10/5 = 2\).
2. Deviations from the means: market \(1, -2, 2, -3, 2\); stock \(1, -4, 3, -3, 3\).
3. Sum of cross products: \(1 + 8 + 6 + 9 + 6 = 30\). Sum of squared market deviations: \(1 + 4 + 4 + 9 + 4 = 22\).
4. Beta: \(30/22 = 1.36\). Intercept: \(2 - 1.36 \times 1 = 0.64\%\) per month.
:::

The reading estimates J.P. Morgan's beta from monthly returns between June 2008 and May 2019, using the SPY ETF as the market: the slope is 0.36 with an \(R^2\) of 0.46, which makes the stock defensive. A **raw beta** comes only from historical data; an **adjusted beta** modifies it, for example to reflect betas' tendency to revert toward 1.

Empirical tests give the CAPM little support. Other factors besides the market drive security returns, which is the subject of [Book 1, Chapter 6](#/b1-06). Beta still matters to corporate managers, because companies often set the hurdle rate for new projects from beta analysis. A hurdle rate set too high makes the company reject worthwhile investments; one set too low leads it to accept poor ones. The original CAPM uses discrete periods such as a month or a year; Robert Merton extended it to continuous time, assuming trading is continuous and prices move without jumps.

## Performance measures

If markets were in equilibrium and stayed there, no investor could earn an abnormal return: every security would lie on the SML and every efficient portfolio on the CML. In reality, expected values can only be estimated with error, and markets are rarely in equilibrium, so realized returns can exceed or fall short of fair compensation for risk. Performance measures compare a portfolio's results with the CAPM relationship between risk and return.

### Sharpe ratio

:::formula Sharpe performance index
$$\text{SPI}=\frac{\E(R_P)-r}{\sigma_P} \qquad \text{benchmark: } \frac{\E(R_M)-r}{\sigma_M}\ \text{(slope of the CML)}$$
- \(\E(R_P), \sigma_P\): mean and standard deviation of the portfolio's returns
:::

The Sharpe ratio is the excess return per unit of total risk. A value above the slope of the CML indicates performance better than equilibrium compensation, and a value below indicates worse.

### Treynor ratio

:::formula Treynor performance index
$$\text{TPI}=\frac{\E(R_P)-r}{\beta_P} \qquad \text{benchmark: } \E(R_M)-r$$
- \(\beta_P\): the portfolio's beta
:::

The Treynor ratio is the excess return per unit of systematic risk. Rearranging the CAPM gives \((\E(R_P)-r)/\beta_P = \E(R_M)-r\) for every asset in equilibrium, so a Treynor ratio above the market risk premium indicates a positive alpha, and one below it a negative alpha. Because it ignores idiosyncratic risk, the Treynor ratio suits portfolios that are part of a well-diversified holding.

### Jensen's alpha

**Jensen's alpha** is the intercept of the market model regression run on the portfolio: the return earned beyond what the CAPM predicts for the portfolio's beta.

:::formula Jensen's performance index (alpha)
$$\alpha_P=\bar R_P-\left[r+\beta_P\left(\bar R_M-r\right)\right]$$
- \(\bar R_P, \bar R_M\): average returns on the portfolio and the market
- In equilibrium \(\alpha_P=0\); a significantly positive alpha indicates superior performance, a negative alpha inferior performance
:::

### How the three measures relate

Dividing the alpha equation by beta links Treynor and Jensen:

:::formula Link between Treynor and Jensen
$$\frac{\bar R_P-r}{\beta_P}=\frac{\alpha_P}{\beta_P}+\left(\bar R_M-r\right)$$
:::

With a positive beta, a Treynor ratio above the market risk premium occurs exactly when alpha is positive, so the two measures agree on whether performance is superior, although they can rank portfolios differently. The Sharpe ratio can disagree with both, because it charges for idiosyncratic risk.

:::example Comparing three portfolios
The risk-free rate is 3%. The market returned 10% with a standard deviation of 15%, so its Sharpe ratio is \(7/15 = 0.467\) and its Treynor ratio is 7%.

| Portfolio | Return | Std. dev. | Beta | Sharpe | Treynor | Jensen's alpha |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P | 11% | 18% | 1.2 | 0.444 | 6.67% | −0.4% |
| Q | 9% | 12% | 0.8 | 0.500 | 7.50% | +0.4% |
| R | 12% | 25% | 1.0 | 0.360 | 9.00% | +2.0% |

1. P: Sharpe \(8/18 = 0.444\); Treynor \(8/1.2 = 6.67\%\); alpha \(11 - (3 + 1.2 \times 7) = -0.4\%\). Below the market on every measure.
2. Q: Sharpe \(6/12 = 0.500\); Treynor \(6/0.8 = 7.50\%\); alpha \(9 - (3 + 0.8 \times 7) = +0.4\%\). Above the market on every measure.
3. R: Treynor \(9/1.0 = 9.00\%\) and alpha \(12 - (3 + 1.0 \times 7) = +2.0\%\) show superior performance, but Sharpe \(9/25 = 0.360\) is below the market's 0.467. With a beta of 1.0, R's systematic variance is \(0.15^2 = 0.0225\) out of a total of \(0.25^2 = 0.0625\), so most of its risk is idiosyncratic.
4. The link holds for R: \(2.0/1.0 + 7 = 9.0\%\), its Treynor ratio.

For an investor whose entire wealth is in R, the Sharpe ratio is the right measure and R looks poor. For an investor who holds R as a small part of a diversified portfolio, its idiosyncratic risk diversifies away, and the Treynor ratio and alpha are the right measures.
:::

### Tracking error and information ratio

The Sharpe and Treynor ratios measure performance against the risk-free rate. The information ratio and the Sortino ratio measure it against a benchmark or target chosen by the client.

:::formula Tracking error and information ratio
$$\text{Active return}_t=R_{Pt}-R_{Bt} \qquad \text{TE}=\SD\left(R_{Pt}-R_{Bt}\right) \qquad \text{IR}=\frac{\text{Average active return}}{\text{TE}}$$
- \(R_{Bt}\): return on the benchmark the portfolio is meant to track or beat
:::

**Tracking error** is the standard deviation of active returns. A portfolio built to replicate its benchmark has a tracking error close to zero, and an active manager's tracking error grows the further holdings depart from the benchmark. The **information ratio** measures how much active return the manager delivers per unit of tracking error.

:::example Tracking error and information ratio
A fund's annual returns minus its benchmark's returns over four years were +2%, −1%, +3%, and 0%.

1. Average active return: \((2 - 1 + 3 + 0)/4 = 1.0\%\).
2. Deviations from the average: \(1, -2, 2, -1\); squared: \(1, 4, 4, 1\), summing to 10.
3. Sample variance: \(10/3 = 3.333\), so tracking error \(= \sqrt{3.333} = 1.826\%\).
4. Information ratio: \(1.0/1.826 = 0.55\).
:::

### Sortino ratio

The **Sortino ratio** modifies the Sharpe ratio for investors who care about downside risk. It replaces the risk-free rate with a **target return** \(T\), also called the **minimum acceptable return (MAR)**, and replaces the standard deviation with the **downside deviation**, which counts only returns below the target.

:::formula Sortino ratio
$$\text{SR}=\frac{\bar R_P-T}{\sqrt{\dfrac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{N}\min\left(0,\,R_{Pt}-T\right)^2}}$$
- \(T\): target or minimum acceptable return, which may be the risk-free rate or another hurdle rate
- \(N\): number of return observations; returns above the target contribute zero to the denominator
:::

:::example Sortino ratio
Monthly returns are 4%, −2%, 2%, 0%, and 3%, and the client's target is 1% a month.

1. Average return: \((4 - 2 + 2 + 0 + 3)/5 = 1.4\%\).
2. Shortfalls below the target, \(\min(0, R_t - 1\%)\): \(0, -3, 0, -1, 0\). Squared: \(0, 9, 0, 1, 0\), summing to 10.
3. Downside deviation: \(\sqrt{10/5} = \sqrt{2} = 1.414\%\).
4. Sortino ratio: \((1.4 - 1.0)/1.414 = 0.28\).
:::

Because it measures performance against a return the client specifies, the Sortino ratio is often more useful to portfolio managers than the Sharpe or Treynor ratio.

| Measure | Reward | Risk | Compared with | Suited to |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sharpe | \(R_P - r\) | Standard deviation | Slope of the CML | A portfolio that is the investor's entire holding |
| Treynor | \(R_P - r\) | Beta | Market risk premium | A portfolio that is one part of diversified holdings |
| Jensen's alpha | Return above the CAPM prediction | Beta, inside the CAPM prediction | Zero | Measuring abnormal return in percentage points |
| Information ratio | Average active return | Tracking error | Benchmark | An active manager judged against a benchmark |
| Sortino | \(R_P - T\) | Downside deviation below \(T\) | Client's target return | An investor concerned with shortfalls below a target |

## Practice questions

:::question
The risk-free rate is 2.5% and the expected market return is 8.5%. What is the CAPM expected return on a stock with a beta of 1.4?
- [x] 10.9%
- [ ] 11.9%
- [ ] 14.4%
- [ ] 8.4%
---
\(\E(R) = 2.5\% + 1.4 \times (8.5\% - 2.5\%) = 2.5\% + 8.4\% = 10.9\%\).

11.9% multiplies beta by the market return instead of the market risk premium. 14.4% adds the risk-free rate to that product. 8.4% is only the risk premium, without the risk-free rate.
:::

:::question
A stock's return has a standard deviation of 40% and a correlation of 0.3 with the market, whose standard deviation is 16%. What is the stock's beta?
- [x] 0.75
- [ ] 2.50
- [ ] 0.30
- [ ] 0.12
---
\(\beta = \rho \times \sigma_i/\sigma_M = 0.3 \times 0.40/0.16 = 0.75\).

2.50 is the ratio of standard deviations without the correlation. 0.30 is the correlation. 0.12 divides the covariance (\(0.3 \times 0.40 \times 0.16 = 0.0192\)) by the market's standard deviation instead of its variance.
:::

:::question
A portfolio has 60% in a stock with beta 1.5 and 40% in a stock with beta 0.5. What is the portfolio's beta?
- [x] 1.10
- [ ] 1.00
- [ ] 0.90
- [ ] 2.00
---
\(\beta_P = 0.60 \times 1.5 + 0.40 \times 0.5 = 0.90 + 0.20 = 1.10\).

1.00 is the unweighted average of the two betas. 0.90 swaps the weights. 2.00 adds the betas.
:::

:::question
The risk-free rate is 3% and the expected market return is 10%. An analyst expects a stock with a beta of 1.2 to return 13%. Which conclusion follows from the CAPM?
- [x] The required return is 11.4%, so the stock is undervalued
- [ ] The required return is 11.4%, so the stock is overvalued
- [ ] The required return is 15.0%, so the stock is overvalued
- [ ] The required return is 8.4%, so the stock is undervalued
---
Required return \(= 3\% + 1.2 \times (10\% - 3\%) = 11.4\%\). The expected 13% is 1.6 percentage points higher, so the stock plots above the security market line: it offers more return than its systematic risk requires, which means its price is too low.

15.0% uses \(3\% + 1.2 \times 10\%\), and 8.4% omits the risk-free rate.
:::

:::question
Portfolio X has a Treynor ratio above the market risk premium but a Sharpe ratio below the market's Sharpe ratio. What best explains this?
- [x] X carries substantial idiosyncratic risk, which the Sharpe ratio penalizes and the Treynor ratio ignores
- [ ] X has a negative beta, which reverses the sign of the Treynor ratio
- [ ] X has a negative Jensen's alpha
- [ ] The Sharpe ratio measures performance against a client benchmark rather than the risk-free rate
---
The Sharpe ratio divides excess return by total risk; the Treynor ratio divides by beta, which captures only systematic risk. A portfolio with a positive alpha but a lot of diversifiable risk scores well on Treynor and poorly on Sharpe. A Treynor ratio above the market risk premium implies a positive alpha when beta is positive, and both ratios use the risk-free rate.
:::

:::question
A portfolio returned 14% with a beta of 1.3. The risk-free rate was 4% and the market returned 11%. What was the portfolio's Jensen's alpha?
- [x] +0.9%
- [ ] +3.0%
- [ ] +10.0%
- [ ] −4.3%
---
CAPM expected return: \(4\% + 1.3 \times (11\% - 4\%) = 13.1\%\). Alpha \(= 14\% - 13.1\% = +0.9\%\).

+3.0% is the portfolio return minus the market return, ignoring beta. +10.0% is the excess return over the risk-free rate. −4.3% multiplies beta by the market return instead of the market risk premium.
:::

:::question
A fund's annual returns minus its benchmark's returns over four years were +1.5%, −0.5%, +2.5%, and +0.5%. Using the sample standard deviation, what are the fund's tracking error and information ratio?
- [x] Tracking error 1.29%; information ratio 0.77
- [ ] Tracking error 1.12%; information ratio 0.89
- [ ] Tracking error 1.00%; information ratio 1.29
- [ ] Tracking error 1.67%; information ratio 0.60
---
Average active return: \((1.5 - 0.5 + 2.5 + 0.5)/4 = 1.0\%\). Squared deviations: \(0.25 + 2.25 + 2.25 + 0.25 = 5.0\). Sample variance \(= 5.0/3 = 1.667\), so tracking error \(= \sqrt{1.667} = 1.29\%\) and IR \(= 1.0/1.29 = 0.77\).

1.12% divides by 4 (the population formula). 1.67% is the variance, not the standard deviation. 1.29 as the IR inverts the ratio.
:::

:::question
Monthly returns on a strategy were 3%, −1%, 2%, −2%, and 3%. The client's minimum acceptable return is 0.5% per month. What is the Sortino ratio?
- [x] 0.38
- [ ] 0.24
- [ ] 0.77
- [ ] 0.50
---
Average return \(= 5/5 = 1.0\%\). Shortfalls below 0.5%: \(0, -1.5, 0, -2.5, 0\); squared \(2.25 + 6.25 = 8.5\). Downside deviation \(= \sqrt{8.5/5} = 1.304\%\). Sortino ratio \(= (1.0 - 0.5)/1.304 = 0.38\).

0.24 divides by the standard deviation of all returns rather than the downside deviation. 0.77 omits the target from the numerator. 0.50 measures shortfalls below 0% instead of below the target.
:::

:::question
Which statement correctly contrasts the capital market line with the security market line?
- [x] The CML measures risk by standard deviation and applies only to efficient portfolios; the SML measures risk by beta and applies to all assets and portfolios
- [ ] The CML measures risk by beta and applies to all assets; the SML measures risk by standard deviation and applies only to efficient portfolios
- [ ] Both lines measure risk by beta, but the CML includes the risk-free asset and the SML does not
- [ ] The SML applies only to the market portfolio, while the CML applies to individual stocks
---
The CML plots expected return against total risk for combinations of the risk-free asset and the market portfolio. The SML plots expected return against systematic risk for any asset; points above it are undervalued and points below it overvalued.
:::

:::question
Studies of mean-variance optimization find that errors in which input distort the optimal portfolio the most?
- [x] Expected returns
- [ ] Variances
- [ ] Covariances
- [ ] The risk-free rate
---
Errors in expected returns are found to be at least ten times as important as errors in variances and covariances, which is why mean-variance portfolios are so sensitive to return forecasts and why techniques such as robust portfolio optimization build estimation error into the process.
:::

:::question
A stock's return has a correlation of 0.7 with the market's return. What share of the stock's return variance is systematic?
- [x] 49%
- [ ] 70%
- [ ] 51%
- [ ] 30%
---
Systematic variance is \(\beta^2\sigma_M^2 = \rho^2\sigma_i^2\), so the systematic share is \(\rho^2 = 0.7^2 = 49\%\), and the idiosyncratic share is 51%. 70% uses the correlation instead of its square.
:::

:::question
Which of the following is not an assumption of the CAPM?
- [x] Investors hold different expectations about asset returns
- [ ] There are no taxes or transaction costs
- [ ] All investors can borrow and lend at a common risk-free rate
- [ ] Investments are perfectly divisible
---
The CAPM assumes homogeneous expectations: every participant has the same expectations, which is why all investors choose the same risky portfolio. The other three statements are CAPM assumptions.
:::

:::question
Four months of excess returns are, for the market, 1%, −2%, 3%, and 2%, and for a stock, 2%, −3%, 4%, and 1%. What is the stock's beta estimated by the market model regression?
- [x] 1.29
- [ ] 0.78
- [ ] 1.00
- [ ] 0.69
---
Both means are 1%. Market deviations: \(0, -3, 2, 1\); stock deviations: \(1, -4, 3, 0\). Sum of cross products \(= 0 + 12 + 6 + 0 = 18\); sum of squared market deviations \(= 0 + 9 + 4 + 1 = 14\). Beta \(= 18/14 = 1.29\).

0.78 inverts the ratio. 1.00 divides the stock's average excess return by the market's. 0.69 divides by the stock's squared deviations (26), which regresses the market on the stock.
:::
