## Why firms manage financial risk

Firms have always managed their core business risks: they study customer demand, keep several suppliers for critical components, back up data, and insure warehouses. Financial risks received the same attention only after two developments:

- **Need.** From the 1970s, commodity, interest rate, credit, and foreign exchange markets were liberalized, price volatility rose sharply, and the global economy expanded.
- **Opportunity.** Volatility created demand for hedging instruments. Theory, such as the Black-Scholes-Merton option pricing model, and securitization technology (pooling loans or receivables and issuing securities backed by the pool) drove rapid growth in instruments through the 1980s and 1990s. Credit and weather derivatives followed in the 1990s, and cyber risk transfer products in the twenty-first century.

OTC derivatives notional amounts grew for two decades and then leveled off in some categories after 2007–2009, as speculative use declined, bank regulation tightened, and interest rates and market volatility fell. Newer risk transfer markets, such as cyber risk, are still growing quickly.

Before managing risk, a firm must decide whether doing so makes sense for its owners, what exactly the program is for, how much risk to retain, which risks to manage, and which instruments and strategies to use. Wrong answers turn the risk management program itself into a major threat to the firm.

### Risk management instruments create risk

Derivatives can change a firm's risk profile within days or hours, either reducing risk or creating a speculative position, and the change may not be visible from outside. Suppose an instrument dampens a firm's exposure to a floating interest rate as long as rates stay within a band, but increases the exposure once rates break through a ceiling: the firm has hedged part of the risk and placed a bet on the rest. With a computer, the right passwords, and board approval, a corporate treasury can take on a risk profile once associated with an investment bank. Part of the resources firms put into risk management exist to make sure that capability is used well.

## Should a firm hedge?

A risk that can be hedged does not necessarily need to be hedged. Hedging stabilizes earnings only over a short horizon of a few years. It has transparent costs, such as an option premium, and less visible ones, such as tactical errors and rogue trading.

### The case against hedging

Finance theory argues that hedging inside the firm adds little for shareholders who hold diversified portfolios:

- **Firm-specific risk** diversifies away in a large portfolio, so reducing the volatility of one stock is worth little to its holders.
- **Systematic risks**, such as interest rate risk, can be managed by investors at the portfolio level, so the firm's hedging duplicates what investors can do for themselves.
- Some investors buy a stock precisely to gain a macroeconomic exposure, such as to oil or gold, and hedging removes the exposure they wanted.

Investors with concentrated holdings, such as family owners or state owners, generally do want the firm to hedge. Decades of empirical studies have not shown conclusively whether hedgers are worth more than non-hedgers, and the answer may differ by industry.

### The case for hedging

The theoretical argument assumes perfect, frictionless markets. Real markets have many imperfections, and hedging addresses several of them:

- **Cost of financial distress.** Distress has direct costs, such as bankruptcy costs, and large opportunity costs: a firm hit by an unexpected market loss cuts investment elsewhere and becomes more cautious.
- **Confidence of creditors, customers, and suppliers.** Creditors get none of the upside from volatile revenue; they care only whether the firm can keep its promises. Key customers and suppliers take the same view.
- **Higher cash flows.** A firm that can offer customers a stable price for three years, which may be impossible without hedging a key input cost, can win more business.
- **Locked-in margins.** A firm committed to supplying a product to a foreign market in one year hedges the currency to fix its profit margin.
- **Planning.** For managers, the most important operational benefit is often the ability to plan when currency or commodity swings would otherwise make budgets meaningless.
- **Other stakeholders.** Managers, regulators, and employees expect the firm to be financially sound and protected from sudden mishaps.

Hedging also opens a channel for **agency risk**: managers may use derivatives to hit short-term targets, such as analysts' earnings expectations, that affect their prestige and pay. A firm should state its reason for hedging in terms of basic aims (managing accounting risk, balance-sheet risk, economic risk, or operational risk) and state the size of its risk appetite.

## The risk management road map

The reading sets out five milestones. The process is iterative: once a firm learns the cost and complexity of managing one business unit's risk, it may reconsider whether to stay in that business at all.

| Milestone | Tasks |
|---|---|
| 1. Identify risk appetite | Identify key corporate goals and risks; decide which risks to manage; write a broad risk appetite statement |
| 2. Map risks and make choices | Map risks and measure their impact; analyze the risk and reward of management strategies (for example with RAROC); compare the costs and benefits of tactics; choose a basic strategy; write a detailed risk appetite statement |
| 3. Operationalize the risk appetite | Express the appetite in operational terms; assess risk policies; set a risk limit framework; rightsize the risk management team (resources, expertise, infrastructure, incentives, independence) |
| 4. Implement | Choose tactics and instruments; make day-to-day decisions; establish oversight |
| 5. Re-evaluate regularly | Capture changes in risk appetite, risk understanding, and stakeholder views; remap business activities and the risk environment; consider new tools, tactics, and cost-benefit analyses |

## Risk appetite

**Risk appetite** is the amount and types of risk a firm is willing to accept in pursuit of its goals. **Risk capacity** is the maximum amount of risk the firm can absorb. The **risk profile** is the risk the firm is exposed to today.

Regulation sets a hard ceiling on a bank's capacity. Its **leverage ratio** may not fall below 3%:

:::formula Basel leverage ratio
$$\text{Leverage ratio}=\frac{\text{Tier 1 capital}}{\text{On-balance-sheet assets}+\text{off-balance-sheet exposures}}\ \ge\ 3\%$$
:::

:::example Leverage ratio as a capacity limit
A bank has Tier 1 capital of USD 36 billion and a total exposure measure of USD 1,000 billion.

1. Leverage ratio \(= 36/1{,}000 = 3.6\%\), above the 3% minimum.
2. Maximum exposure at 3%: \(36/0.03 = 1{,}200\), or USD 1,200 billion.
3. Without raising capital, the bank can add at most USD 200 billion of exposure before breaching the minimum. Its risk appetite should sit well inside that capacity.
:::

In practical terms, a risk appetite has two parts:

1. **A statement** of the firm's willingness to take risk in pursuit of its business goals. The detailed statement is usually an internal document approved by the board; shortened versions appear in some annual reports.
2. **The mechanisms** that link the statement to day-to-day risk management: the detailed risk policy, risk statements for individual businesses, and the framework of limits for key risk areas.

The board should also approve the operational version of the appetite, and that version must be consistent with the other risk signals the firm sends staff, especially incentive compensation. Banks, pushed by regulators and by crises, lead in developing the concept. HSBC's 2016 annual report describes a risk appetite statement that sets quantitative and qualitative metrics in 13 categories, including earnings, capital and leverage, liquidity and funding, interest rate risk in the banking book, credit risk, traded risk, operational risk, financial crime compliance, and regulatory compliance. Measuring the business against those metrics guides business activity, informs risk-adjusted pay, tests planning assumptions, and flags decisions needed to reduce risk. The board approves the statement on the advice of its risk committee, and each global business, region, and function writes its own aligned statement during annual planning.

### Appetite, capacity, and profile

"Risk appetite" is used loosely for three different amounts: the risk the firm could bear without becoming insolvent (capacity), the risk it is taking today (profile), and the risk it would be content to bear at any one time (appetite). In a well-built framework:

- Appetite is set well below capacity.
- The current profile sits below appetite.
- Upper and lower **trigger points** around the appetite alert the board when the firm is close to breaching it, or when risk taking looks unnaturally low.

A firm's own risk appetite is a fairly stable, through-the-cycle attitude. The "corporate risk appetite" measured by business surveys, such as surveys of chief financial officers, is different: a point-in-time reading of sentiment across an industry, driven largely by external events such as Brexit or changes in GDP growth.

Appetite can differ by risk type, because it depends on the firm's expertise. A technology firm may adopt a very high-risk strategy because falling behind competitors would end its business; for that firm, taking the bet is risk management. The same firm can take a very conservative approach to foreign exchange risk and manage cyber risk more skillfully than a firm that calls itself conservative. Risk appetite is part of a firm's identity, so it has to answer "Who are we?" and "Who do our stakeholders think we are?" before it can put an appetite into operation.

### Putting risk appetite into operation

No single measure of risk can be monitored at the business level and then added up to the enterprise level, so linking a board-level statement to operating metrics is hard. Firms use several measures together: notional limits by business and risk type, estimates of unexpected loss, versions of VaR, and stress tests, with the level of detail matched to the nature of the risk and the sophistication of the strategy.

## Mapping risks

A risk appetite statement sets the objective. **Risk mapping** identifies the key exposures at the level of cash flows, with their size and timing over relevant horizons:

- **Commodity exposure.** A manufacturer that uses copper needs to know how much metal it must hold, when it needs it, where it must be delivered, and which local price benchmark best represents its cost.
- **Foreign exchange exposure.** Map existing positions, contracts, and upcoming transactions; set a policy on which exposures to hedge, such as whether to include sales that are probable but not yet certain; lay out the timing of cash flows; and identify assets and liabilities exposed to exchange rates.
- **Natural offsets.** Mapping shows where cash flows cancel each other, by design or by accident, so the firm can count the netting and diversification and plan to increase it.
- **Insurable and hard-to-track risks.** Natural catastrophes, physical accidents, and cyber incidents belong on the map, as do risks without clear cash flows, such as the data privacy exposure created by a new business line.

## Choosing a strategy

With the appetite set and risks mapped, the risk manager ranks exposures by severity and urgency, then compares the costs and benefits of four strategies:

| Strategy | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Retain** | Accept a risk entirely, or accept part of its loss distribution. Retained risks are not necessarily small. | A gold miner keeps its gold price exposure because its investors want it; an input price risk that behaves like expected loss is priced into the product |
| **Avoid** | Remove risks the firm regards as unnatural to its business, sometimes possible only by stopping an activity | A "zero tolerance" policy toward a risky behavior, which describes reality only if safeguards enforce it |
| **Mitigate** | Reduce exposure, frequency, or severity | Requiring more collateral against a credit exposure; an airline buying fuel-efficient aircraft to reduce its jet fuel price exposure |
| **Transfer** | Move part of the risk to a third party, at a cost | Insurance, financial derivatives, securitization |

Senior management and the board choose strategies for the largest risks; the risk manager's job is to show which option keeps the firm within its appetite most efficiently. The costs of each option are rarely fully visible:

- The cost of a transfer should include the cost of the staff who run it and of managing the risk left over, such as basis risk.
- A firm that hedges a commodity price loses ground to unhedged competitors when the spot price falls, a competitive cost that is hard to put a number on.
- Hard-to-quantify risks need worst-case analysis and expert judgment. A firm might estimate a 2% annual chance of a USD 250 million data breach loss, then compare an expensive systems upgrade (mitigation) with cyber insurance (transfer).

## Rightsizing the risk management function

The risk management function has to be fit for the strategy. The firm needs to decide:

- Its risk appetite and hedging philosophy.
- Its basic goals, such as reducing earnings volatility, improving how markets perceive its soundness, reducing taxes paid, or reducing the chance of breaching limits.
- The accounting treatment of risk management: cost center, economic center, or profit center. Risk management at most non-financial firms is a cost center, while some parts of risk management at banks run as profit centers. A related decision is whether to charge risk management costs to the areas it serves.
- Which risk types and time horizons the function covers.
- Its resources and budget.
- Reporting lines, accountability, oversight, and independence.
- Documented policies and procedures.
- How performance is evaluated and how incentive pay works.

A one-off hedge or an annual insurance purchase needs little infrastructure. A **dynamic hedging** strategy, readjusted continually in the markets, can save costs but needs larger investments in systems and trader expertise, complex models, metrics such as VaR, a wider limit system, and a clear separation of the trading function from the back office and risk oversight. A firm without that capacity becomes dependent on its dealers, such as investment banks, and may be unable to price its own instruments independently. Several times a year the board should check for gaps between the sophistication of the hedging program and the conservatism of the stated strategy.

### Risk limits

| Limit | What it caps | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| **Stop-loss** | Losses; reaching the threshold triggers an action such as closing the position or escalating | Caps realized losses but does not prevent new exposure |
| **Notional** | The size of an exposure | Notional amounts can bear little relation to the economic risk of derivatives, especially options |
| **Risk-specific** | A feature of a particular risk, such as a liquidity ratio for liquidity risk | Hard to aggregate; needs specialist knowledge to interpret |
| **Maturity or gap** | The volume of transactions maturing or repricing in each period | Reduces the operational and liquidity strain of bunched maturities but does not address price risk |
| **Concentration** | Exposure to one counterparty, product type, or other grouping | Must reflect correlation, which may not behave as expected in stressed markets |
| **Greeks** | Option sensitivities such as delta, gamma, and vega | Subject to model risk; calculations can be compromised at desk level without independent controls |
| **VaR** | An aggregate statistical loss estimate | Subject to model risk; senior management can misread it; says nothing about losses in unusually stressed markets |
| **Stress, sensitivity, and scenario** | Losses in plausible worst cases: specific stresses, sensitivity to key variables, historical or hypothetical scenarios | Quality depends on deep knowledge of exposures and market behavior; the number of possible scenarios is endless |

## The risk transfer toolbox

| Instrument | Defining features |
|---|---|
| **Forward** | A tailored agreement to exchange a set quantity of an asset at a set price on a future date, settled by physical delivery or in cash |
| **Futures** | An exchange-listed forward with standardized terms and margining |
| **Swap** | An OTC agreement to exchange the cash flows of two economic positions until maturity, such as fixed against floating interest on a notional amount |
| **Call option** | The right, but not the obligation, to buy at the strike price at maturity (European) or at any time before it (American) |
| **Put option** | The right, but not the obligation, to sell at the strike price at maturity (European) or at any time before it (American) |
| **Exotic option** | Options beyond standard calls and puts, such as Asian (average price) options and basket options |
| **Swaption** | The right, but not the obligation, to enter a swap on pre-agreed terms at a future date |

Choosing an instrument is partly a decision about how much to pay for flexibility. A forward gives price stability but no flexibility, because the transaction must happen at the agreed time and price. A purchased option gives price stability and keeps the benefit of favorable moves, at the cost of the premium.

:::formula Hedged proceeds from a foreign currency receivable
$$\text{Forward hedge: } Q \times F \qquad\quad \text{Put option hedge: } Q \times \max(S_T, K) - \text{premium}$$
- \(Q\): foreign currency amount to be received
- \(F\): forward rate agreed today; \(S_T\): spot rate on the receipt date; \(K\): strike rate of the put
:::

:::example Forward versus option for a euro receivable
A US exporter will receive EUR 10 million in one year. The one-year forward rate is USD 1.10 per euro. A one-year put on euros with a strike of 1.10 costs USD 0.03 per euro, or USD 0.3 million in total (ignore interest on the premium).

| Spot rate in one year | Unhedged | Forward hedge | Put hedge |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.00 | USD 10.0m | USD 11.0m | \(11.0 - 0.3 =\) USD 10.7m |
| 1.10 | USD 11.0m | USD 11.0m | \(11.0 - 0.3 =\) USD 10.7m |
| 1.20 | USD 12.0m | USD 11.0m | \(12.0 - 0.3 =\) USD 11.7m |

The forward removes all uncertainty, including the gain if the euro strengthens. The put sets a floor of USD 10.7 million and keeps the upside, in exchange for the USD 0.3 million premium.
:::

### Exchange-traded and OTC instruments

Exchange-traded derivatives are designed to attract trading, so most can be traded easily at low transaction cost, and margin requirements and netting keep counterparty credit exposure small. The drawback resembles buying a suit off the rack: the standard contract may not cover the exact risk, may mature at the wrong time, or may reference a price at the wrong location, and each mismatch creates **basis risk**. OTC contracts are tailored to the exposure but carry counterparty credit risk, which tends to look small until a crisis reveals fragile counterparties. Clearing houses now handle a larger share of OTC contracts, which has narrowed the counterparty risk difference between the two.

### Hedging commodity inputs: brewers

Modern risk management arguably began with the agricultural futures listed on the Chicago Board of Trade in the 1860s. A large brewer such as Anheuser-Busch is exposed to the prices of wheat, barley, hops, corn products, the aluminum in its cans, and energy, and it uses both kinds of instrument:

- **Wheat through exchange futures.** The brewer buys futures to fix the price per bushel and holds them for some months. Near maturity it either takes delivery on the exchange's terms or sells the contracts and uses the proceeds to buy from its preferred supplier. Either way, a liquid exchange contract has largely fixed the wheat price for that period.
- **Aluminum through an OTC swap.** The brewer pays a bank a fixed price for a set quantity of aluminum every few weeks, and the bank pays the brewer the variable price charged by the brewer's local suppliers. Matching the swap's dates and price reference to the brewer's actual purchases removes the timing and location basis risk a standard contract would leave. The bank hedges its own position using its metals market expertise, and the brewer is protected from price spikes caused by events such as tariffs or sanctions on major producers.

:::formula Net price under a pay-fixed commodity swap
$$\underbrace{S}_{\text{paid to supplier}}+\underbrace{(X-S)}_{\text{net swap payment}}=X$$
- \(S\): floating market price paid to the supplier
- \(X\): fixed swap price; when \(S > X\) the bank pays the brewer \(S - X\), and when \(S < X\) the brewer pays the bank \(X - S\)
:::

### Hedging jet fuel: airlines

Jet fuel accounts for as much as 15–20% of an airline's operating costs, and fares follow passenger demand rather than costs, so airlines cannot pass fuel price spikes on to customers. Since the mid-1980s airlines have used swaps, call options, collars (a bought call combined with a sold put), futures, and forward contracts on jet fuel. The market matured quickly after energy prices spiked during the 1990–1991 Gulf War.

Few futures contracts exist on jet fuel itself, so airlines often **cross-hedge** with crude oil or heating oil futures. The cross-hedge leaves them exposed to the volatile spread between jet fuel and crude oil prices, and to timing and location basis. Many airlines instead use OTC contracts tailored to jet fuel prices and their own delivery requirements.

:::example Basis risk in a crude oil cross-hedge
An airline expects to buy 2 million barrels of jet fuel and buys crude oil futures on 2 million barrels. Over the hedge period, jet fuel rises from USD 95 to USD 120 a barrel and crude oil rises from USD 78 to USD 90.

1. Higher fuel cost: \((120 - 95) \times 2\text{ million} =\) USD 50 million.
2. Futures gain: \((90 - 78) \times 2\text{ million} =\) USD 24 million.
3. Net increase in fuel cost: \(50 - 24 =\) USD 26 million.

The jet fuel–crude oil spread widened from USD 17 to USD 30 a barrel, and that USD 13 widening on 2 million barrels is exactly the USD 26 million the hedge failed to cover.
:::

The industry still disagrees about whether airlines should hedge. Most hedge part of their fuel costs, and some hedge none:

- **Against hedging:** programs are expensive, and a hedge can lock in a high fuel price just before a steep fall, producing hedging losses and making the airline look less profitable than unhedged rivals. Unhedged American Airlines took full advantage of a 40–50% fall in jet fuel prices in 2014 and saved about USD 600 million relative to hedged competitors, several of which then cut back their hedging.
- **For hedging:** staying unhedged is also a bet. In 2008 oil prices reached unexpected highs during the financial crisis, and the airlines with the tightest hedging programs came out best.

Hedging only part of the fuel bill and using options are compromises; option strategies are arguably the purest form of risk management but are expensive. Delta Air Lines bought an oil refinery in 2012 as part of its fuel strategy, which has helped it secure jet fuel supply in a key region and cover the spread between jet fuel and crude oil costs. Vertical integration has drawbacks: refining has its own profit cycles, running a refinery can distract management, and because most of a refinery's output is not jet fuel, owning one can increase the airline's exposure to crude oil price volatility.

### Interest rate and foreign exchange risk

A global business such as McDonald's, which operates in more than 100 countries, faces both risks. Its 2017 annual report describes its approach. It generally borrows long term, using capital markets, bank financing, and derivatives to meet financing needs and reduce interest expense. It manages its debt portfolio by retiring, redeeming, and repurchasing debt, terminating swaps, and using derivatives; all its swaps are OTC, and it holds no derivatives for trading. It borrows in the currencies in which its assets are denominated, and it uses foreign currency debt and derivatives to hedge royalties, intercompany financing, and investments in foreign subsidiaries. Its restaurants buy goods and services in local currencies, which creates **natural hedges**. Large firms get much of their protection from natural offsets and business decisions, and many use derivatives only to fine-tune the result.

Borrowing in the same currency as foreign operations reduces currency risk, but it is not a practical option in every market. For interest rate risk, the task is to avoid taking on too much debt at high rates and too much exposure to floating rates. The balance depends on the firm's financial risk appetite (the levels of debt the board accepts) and on the proportion of fixed-rate to floating-rate debt across horizons, and it must be consistent with the firm's target credit rating and its loan covenants.

Factors that shape a treasurer's interest rate risk management:

| Factor | Effect on decisions |
|---|---|
| Firm risk appetite | Sets the key goals |
| Market practicalities | It may be easier to raise money in one market and use derivatives to change its currency or its fixed or floating character |
| Changing business and financing needs | Debt matures and rolls over; businesses grow |
| Cost center or profit center | A profit center treasurer may be permitted to take a view on market direction |
| Regulation and taxes | The treasurer must respond to changes in the rules |
| Market direction and behavior | The treasurer may need to prepare for rising rates or respond to shifts in the yield curve (yield curve risk) |

Rates also interact with the business itself, because they move with the economy and consumer demand. When rates fall in a downturn, floating-rate debt becomes cheaper to service just as revenue weakens, which is a natural hedge.

:::formula Effective rate after a pay-fixed interest rate swap
$$\underbrace{(\text{SOFR}+s)}_{\text{loan interest}}+\underbrace{R_{\text{fixed}}}_{\text{paid on swap}}-\underbrace{\text{SOFR}}_{\text{received on swap}}=R_{\text{fixed}}+s$$
- \(s\): credit spread on the floating-rate loan
- \(R_{\text{fixed}}\): fixed rate paid on the swap
:::

:::example Fixing the cost of floating-rate debt
A company pays SOFR + 1.5% on USD 100 million of debt and enters a swap to pay 3.5% fixed and receive SOFR.

| SOFR | Unhedged cost | Hedged cost |
|---|---|---|
| 2.0% | 3.5% | 5.0% |
| 6.0% | 7.5% | 5.0% |

The swap fixes the cost at 5.0%, or USD 5.0 million a year, whatever SOFR does, and gives up the saving if rates fall.
:::

## What goes wrong in corporate hedging

A firm can misunderstand the type of risk it faces, map or measure it incorrectly, miss a change in market structure, or employ a rogue trader. Two failure patterns deserve separate attention:

- **Programs not intended to manage risk.** Swaps and other derivatives can reduce the interest a firm pays now in exchange for much more downside risk, or for small payments today and ballooning payments later. Such programs usually aim to meet analysts' forecasts or cover up business problems, and they often involve needlessly complex structures, leverage, or strategies that fail after an unlikely but plausible event, such as a sudden move in interest rates or widening basis. The failure is one of corporate governance rather than risk management.
- **Poor communication** of the hedging strategy and its possible consequences.

Guidelines for conservative end users:

- Set clear goals.
- Keep instruments and strategies simple.
- Disclose the strategy and explain its consequences.
- Match resources and limits to the strategy.
- Stress test and set up early warning indicators.
- Watch for counterparty risk and break clauses.
- Work through the consequences of many market scenarios, including margin calls.

### Metallgesellschaft (MGRM), 1993

MG Refining and Marketing (MGRM), the US energy trading subsidiary of Metallgesellschaft AG, agreed to supply customers with 150 million barrels of gasoline and heating oil over ten years at fixed prices. Those contracts lost value if oil prices rose, so MGRM hedged with a very large position in short-dated futures and OTC swaps that it rolled forward as each contract expired (a **stack-and-roll** hedge).

1. Spot oil prices fell, and the futures curve moved from **backwardation** (later-dated contracts cheaper than near-dated ones) to **contango** (later-dated contracts more expensive).
2. Falling prices produced large margin calls on the long futures, payable in cash immediately, while the offsetting gains on the fixed-price supply contracts would arrive only as oil was delivered over ten years. In contango, each roll also meant selling the expiring contract for less than the next contract cost.
3. The parent company, alarmed by the cash drain, closed out the hedges at a considerable loss.
4. The market then reversed and prices rose, so the now-unhedged fixed-price supply contracts lost money.

MGRM lost twice: once when it unwound the hedges and again on the unhedged customer commitments. No rogue trader was involved, and the hedge might have worked if it had been kept to the end. Had management anticipated the liquidity effect of hedging with futures and explained it to stakeholders, the firm could have set aside funds to meet the margin calls, or chosen a hedge that generated less collateral pressure.

:::example Why a sound futures hedge can drain cash
A firm has agreed to sell oil to customers at fixed prices for ten years and hedges with long futures on 150 million barrels. Near-term oil prices fall by USD 4 a barrel.

1. Variation margin due on the futures: \(150\text{ million} \times 4 =\) USD 600 million, payable within days.
2. The fixed-price sales become more valuable as prices fall, but that value turns into cash only as oil is delivered over the ten years.
3. The hedge can be economically sound and still require USD 600 million of cash now. A firm that cannot fund the margin is forced to close the hedge.
:::

## Risk culture

Limits and instruments work only in a culture where everyone pursues the same risk goals. Evidence that a firm's culture supports its risk management:

- It communicates regularly about risk and responds to warning signs and near misses.
- It has tested whether key staff share a common understanding of the risk appetite.
- Its board is aware of the firm's top ten risks.

A risk manager who meets an agreed goal, such as stabilizing a volatile business exposure over three years, has done a difficult job, and that success should form part of a strategy already explained to stakeholders and supported by the whole firm. In a 2018 ISDA survey, most derivatives professionals expected end users' derivatives activity to increase over the following three to five years.

## Practice questions

:::question
Which description matches a bank's risk capacity rather than its risk appetite or risk profile?
- [ ] The amount and types of risk the board is willing to accept in pursuit of its strategy
- [x] The maximum amount of risk the bank can absorb before breaching its solvency or regulatory minimums
- [ ] The amount of risk the bank is exposed to today
- [ ] The level of risk at which the board receives an early warning report
---
Risk capacity is the most risk the firm can bear; for a bank it is bounded by constraints such as the 3% minimum leverage ratio. Risk appetite, the amount of risk the firm is content to bear, is set below capacity. The risk profile is the current exposure, and trigger points around the appetite generate early warning reports.
:::

:::question
Most of a company's shares are held by diversified investors. Which argument best supports hedging at the company level despite that?
- [x] Hedging lowers the probability of financial distress and its direct and indirect costs
- [ ] Hedging removes firm-specific risk that diversified investors cannot eliminate themselves
- [ ] Hedging raises expected cash flows by the amount of the premium paid
- [ ] Diversified investors have no way to manage interest rate risk at the portfolio level
---
Market imperfections make financial distress costly, through bankruptcy costs and the investment a distressed firm forgoes, and hedging reduces the chance of reaching that point. Diversified investors can eliminate firm-specific risk themselves and can manage systematic risks such as interest rate risk in their portfolios, which is the theoretical case against hedging. Paying a premium reduces cash flows rather than raising them.
:::

:::question
A US company will receive EUR 5 million in six months. The six-month forward rate is USD 1.08 per euro, and a six-month put on euros with a strike of 1.08 costs USD 0.02 per euro. If the spot rate in six months is 1.15, what are the dollar proceeds under a forward hedge and under a put hedge? Ignore interest on the premium.
- [x] Forward USD 5.40 million; put USD 5.65 million
- [ ] Forward USD 5.75 million; put USD 5.40 million
- [ ] Forward USD 5.40 million; put USD 5.30 million
- [ ] Forward USD 5.40 million; put USD 5.75 million
---
Forward: \(5 \times 1.08 = 5.40\) million regardless of the spot rate.

Put: spot (1.15) is above the strike (1.08), so the company lets the put expire and sells euros at spot: \(5 \times 1.15 = 5.75\) million, minus the premium of \(5 \times 0.02 = 0.10\) million, for USD 5.65 million.

USD 5.30 million wrongly exercises the put at 1.08 and subtracts the premium. USD 5.75 million forgets the premium.
:::

:::question
A company pays SOFR + 1.20% on USD 50 million of floating-rate debt. It enters a swap to pay 3.80% fixed and receive SOFR. What is its effective borrowing rate if SOFR rises to 5.00%?
- [x] 5.00%
- [ ] 6.20%
- [ ] 3.80%
- [ ] 10.00%
---
Loan interest \((5.00\% + 1.20\%)\), plus fixed swap payment \((3.80\%)\), minus floating swap receipt \((5.00\%)\), equals \(3.80\% + 1.20\% = 5.00\%\). The SOFR terms cancel, leaving the fixed rate plus the loan spread.

6.20% is the unhedged cost. 3.80% ignores the loan spread. 10.00% adds the swap payment without subtracting the SOFR received.

:::question
A gold mining company decides not to hedge its gold price exposure because its shareholders buy the stock to gain exposure to gold. Which risk management strategy is it following?
- [x] Retain
- [ ] Avoid
- [ ] Mitigate
- [ ] Transfer
---
The company keeps the risk deliberately, which is retention. Retained risks need not be small: this one is the company's largest exposure, kept because investors want it. Avoiding gold price risk would mean leaving the business, mitigating would reduce the exposure, and transferring would pass it to a third party through derivatives or other contracts.
:::

:::question
Compared with a tailored OTC forward, an exchange-traded futures contract used as a hedge most likely has:
- [x] Lower counterparty credit risk and more basis risk
- [ ] Higher counterparty credit risk and less basis risk
- [ ] Lower counterparty credit risk and less basis risk
- [ ] Higher counterparty credit risk and more basis risk
---
Margin requirements and netting keep counterparty exposure on exchange-traded futures small. Their standardized terms rarely match the hedger's exact risk, timing, and location, which creates basis risk. A tailored OTC forward removes those mismatches but exposes the hedger to the counterparty's credit.
:::

:::question
Which type of risk limit is least able to capture the economic risk of a portfolio of options?
- [x] Notional limits
- [ ] Greek limits on delta, gamma, and vega
- [ ] Stress test limits
- [ ] Value-at-risk limits
---
The notional amount of an option can bear little relation to its risk: a far out-of-the-money option and an at-the-money option with the same notional have very different sensitivities to the underlying. Greek limits measure those sensitivities directly, and VaR and stress limits estimate potential losses, although each has its own model risk.
:::

:::question
Which statement best describes the losses at Metallgesellschaft's US subsidiary (MGRM) in 1993?
- [x] Falling oil prices produced margin calls on its long futures while gains on its fixed-price supply contracts would arrive only over ten years; the parent closed the hedges at a loss before prices rose against the unhedged contracts
- [ ] A rogue trader hid losses on unauthorized positions in an error account until they became too large to conceal
- [ ] Rising oil prices caused losses on short futures positions that MGRM had used to hedge its supply contracts
- [ ] The hedge failed because no exchange-traded futures existed on gasoline or heating oil
---
MGRM sold oil products forward at fixed prices and hedged with long, rolled futures and swaps. Falling prices and a shift to contango created a large cash drain from margin calls, the parent unwound the hedges at a loss, and prices then rose against the now-unhedged commitments, so the firm lost twice. No rogue trader was involved, and the hedges were long, not short.
:::

:::question
An airline expects to buy 2 million barrels of jet fuel and hedges with crude oil futures on 2 million barrels. Jet fuel rises from USD 95 to USD 120 a barrel while crude oil rises from USD 78 to USD 90. What is the net increase in the airline's fuel cost after the hedge?
- [x] USD 26 million
- [ ] USD 0
- [ ] USD 50 million
- [ ] USD 74 million
---
Fuel cost rises by \(25 \times 2 = 50\) million. The futures gain \(12 \times 2 = 24\) million. The net increase is USD 26 million, equal to the USD 13 widening of the jet fuel–crude spread on 2 million barrels.

USD 0 assumes a perfect hedge, USD 50 million ignores the hedge, and USD 74 million adds the futures gain to the cost increase.
:::

:::question
Which hedging motive reflects agency risk rather than shareholder value?
- [x] Using derivatives to smooth reported earnings so that the firm meets the analyst forecasts on which managers' bonuses depend
- [ ] Hedging a key input cost so that the firm can offer customers stable prices for three years
- [ ] Hedging to reduce the probability of financial distress
- [ ] Hedging foreign currency receivables so that the firm can plan production and investment
---
Agency risk arises when managers act in their own interest, such as protecting bonuses tied to short-term targets, rather than the owners' interest. The other three motives raise cash flows, lower distress costs, or improve planning, all of which can benefit shareholders.
:::

:::question
Delta Air Lines bought an oil refinery in 2012 as part of its fuel strategy. What is a drawback of this form of vertical integration as a hedge?
- [x] Most of a refinery's output is not jet fuel, so owning one can add exposure to crude oil prices and refining profit cycles
- [ ] It removes Delta's ability to secure jet fuel supply in a key region
- [ ] It transfers fuel price risk to a third party at a fixed, known cost
- [ ] It creates daily margin calls like an exchange-traded futures hedge
---
A refinery covers the spread between jet fuel and crude oil and helps secure supply, but it brings exposure to products other than jet fuel, to the refining industry's own cycles, and to management distraction. It does not transfer risk to a third party, and owning a physical asset does not create margin calls.
:::

:::question
A corporate treasury moves from a single annual forward hedge to a dynamic hedging program adjusted in the market every day. Which change is most necessary?
- [x] Investment in systems, models, and a wider limit framework, with the trading function separated from back-office and risk oversight
- [ ] Reclassifying risk management as a profit center so that traders can take views
- [ ] Dropping risk limits, since positions change too often for limits to apply
- [ ] Relying on the dealer banks' valuations of the positions
---
Dynamic strategies can reduce hedging costs but require more systems, trader expertise, models, metrics such as VaR, limits, and a clear separation of trading from settlement and oversight. Relying on dealers' valuations is the dependence a rightsized function is meant to avoid, and a dynamic program needs more limits, not fewer.
