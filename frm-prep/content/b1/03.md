## Corporate governance and risk governance

**Corporate governance** is the way a company is run: the roles and responsibilities of its shareholders, board of directors, and senior management. **Risk governance** is the part of it that covers how a firm takes and oversees risk. It sets up the organizational infrastructure (committees, reporting lines, and formal procedures for defining, implementing, and overseeing risk management) and the channels that make risk transparent inside the firm and to regulators and other stakeholders. Over two decades risk governance moved from a vague principle to a defined set of best practices at the center of banking regulation, pushed by two waves of failure.

## Accounting scandals and the Sarbanes-Oxley Act

The first wave was accounting fraud: Enron (2001), WorldCom and Global Crossing (2002), and Parmalat (2003). Executives committed the fraud, but auditors and boards failed to stop it, and boards and shareholders were not told about the economic risks management had taken. Derivatives and other engineered products were sometimes used to hide how weak the firms' finances were.

The United States responded with legislation. The **Sarbanes-Oxley Act (SOX)** was passed in 2002, and in 2003 the SEC required exchanges to bring their listing standards into line with it. SOX set stricter legal duties for boards, senior management, and external and internal auditors:

- The **chief executive officer (CEO)** and **chief financial officer (CFO)** of a public company must certify that reports filed with the SEC contain no untrue statement of a material fact and omit no material fact, and that disclosures present the company's financial condition and operations completely and accurately.
- The CEO and CFO are responsible for designing and maintaining internal controls.
- Officers must disclose significant deficiencies in internal controls, and any fraud by people with a material role in the control system, to the external auditors, the internal audit function, and the audit committee.
- The effectiveness of reporting procedures and controls must be reviewed every year.
- The names of audit committee members must be disclosed. Members are expected to understand accounting principles, read financial statements, have internal audit experience, and understand the audit committee's functions.

Europe did not legislate. Regulators reformed voluntary corporate governance codes under a **comply-or-explain** regime, in which a company follows the code or explains publicly why it departs from it. Those reforms covered internal controls, governance mechanisms, and financial disclosure, and did not directly address risk management.

## The 2007–2009 crisis and bank governance

The second wave was a failure of risk management itself. A booming US housing market, fed by easy mortgage lending, turned down. Lenders had extended mortgages to unqualified borrowers and encouraged homeowners to borrow more than they could repay. Investment banks packaged the loans into complex securities, including privately issued mortgage-backed securities with no government backing, which spread through the credit markets. The originators, the traders, and the rating agencies all misjudged the securities' value and risk. As defaults rose, major investment banks holding low-quality assets came close to collapse. During the boom, risk management at many institutions had been sidelined while executives chased returns, underwriting standards had fallen, and oversight had broken down. The crisis started in the United States but was systemic and global.

Neither SOX nor Europe's light-touch codes prevented it. Inquiries found that banks had paid little attention to tail risk and genuine worst cases, and the debate turned to how banks in particular should be governed:

| Issue | Post-crisis concern |
|---|---|
| **Stakeholder priority** | Banks are funded by deposits, debt, and implicit government guarantees as well as equity. Depositors, debtholders, and taxpayers care far more about avoiding failure than shareholders, who often press for short-term results, so giving shareholders more power, the usual governance remedy, may not fix banks. |
| **Board composition** | Boards need independence, engagement, and financial industry expertise. Analyses of failed banks show no clear link between failure and a majority of either insiders or outsiders; Northern Rock, which failed, had several banking experts on its board. |
| **Board risk oversight** | Boards must oversee risk proactively, which means educating directors about risk and giving the board a direct line to the risk function, for example by having the chief risk officer (CRO) report directly to the board. |
| **Risk appetite** | Regulators pushed banks to adopt a formal, board-approved risk appetite stating the firm's willingness to take risk and tolerate threats to solvency, translated into firm-wide limits, with the board involved in setting them. |
| **Compensation** | Pay is one of the board's main levers on risk behavior. Boards must examine how pay structures affect risk taking and whether risk adjustments capture long-term risks; reforms include limiting bonuses, deferring them, and adding clawbacks. |

## The regulatory response

### The Basel Accords

The **Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS)** brings together central banks and bank supervisors from 27 jurisdictions to set international standards for prudential bank regulation. The standards are not legally binding; jurisdictions write them into their own rules.

| Accord | Trigger | Main content |
|---|---|---|
| **Basel I** (1988) | The Latin American debt crisis of the 1980s | A uniform capital standard focused on credit risk: minimum capital of 8% of **risk-weighted assets** |
| **Basel II** (work began 1999, finalized 2006) | Basel I's crude risk weights | Kept the 8% minimum with refined, more risk-sensitive weights; brought trading activity into the capital calculation alongside lending; added supervisory review standards and disclosure requirements to strengthen market discipline |
| **Basel III** (after 2007–2009) | Banks' lack of resilience in a system-wide crisis | Addresses firm-specific and **systemic risk**, the risk that one major institution's failure brings down interconnected institutions and harms the economy: better capital, liquidity ratios, wider risk coverage, and a macroprudential overlay |

:::example Basel I minimum capital
A bank has USD 500 billion of risk-weighted assets.

1. Minimum capital \(= 0.08 \times 500 =\) USD 40 billion.
2. With USD 36 billion of capital, the ratio is \(36/500 = 7.2\%\), below the minimum.
3. The bank must raise USD 4 billion of capital or reduce risk-weighted assets to \(36/0.08 =\) USD 450 billion.
:::

Basel III made these changes:

- **Capital quality.** Core Tier 1 capital is limited to common equity and retained earnings, which absorb losses, unlike hybrid debt.
- **Liquidity.** The **liquidity coverage ratio (LCR)** covers a 30-day stress period and the **net stable funding ratio (NSFR)** covers one year. The NSFR counters pro-cyclicality by reducing banks' reliance on short-term wholesale funding, which disappears in a credit crisis and forces banks to sell assets at depressed prices.
- **Wider risk coverage.** Capital rules now capture exposures to central counterparties, margin on derivatives that are not centrally cleared, counterparty credit risk, and securitization.
- **A macroprudential overlay** to reduce systemic risk and pro-cyclicality, with five elements:
  1. A **leverage ratio** of 3%.
  2. A **countercyclical capital buffer**.
  3. **Total loss-absorbing capacity (TLAC)** standards for global systemically important banks (G-SIBs).
  4. Rules for systemically important markets and infrastructures, including moving as many OTC derivatives as possible to central clearing and trade reporting.
  5. Capturing systemic risk and tail events in risk models and stress tests.

The **Fundamental Review of the Trading Book (FRTB)** revised the market risk framework in 2016 and expanded disclosure so that risk calculations are more comprehensive and comparable across banks.

### Basel corporate governance principles

The BCBS issued bank governance principles in October 2010, covering board duties, board qualifications, and an independent risk function, and revised them in July 2015 to strengthen the board's active role in oversight. The 2015 version sets out the roles of the board, board risk committees, senior management, CROs, and internal auditors in 13 principles:

| # | Principle | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Board's overall responsibilities | The board is responsible for the bank, including approving and overseeing management's implementation of strategy, governance framework, and corporate culture |
| 2 | Board qualifications and composition | Members are qualified individually and collectively, understand their oversight role, and exercise sound, objective judgment |
| 3 | Board's own structure and practices | The board defines governance structures and practices for its own work and reviews them periodically |
| 4 | Senior management | Runs the bank consistently with the strategy, risk appetite, remuneration, and other policies the board approved |
| 5 | Governance of group structures | The parent board is responsible for the whole group and for a governance framework suited to its structure, businesses, and risks |
| 6 | Risk management function | An effective, independent function under a CRO with sufficient stature, independence, resources, and access to the board |
| 7 | Risk identification, monitoring, and controlling | Done continuously, bank-wide and entity by entity; sophistication keeps pace with the risk profile, external risks, and industry practice |
| 8 | Risk communication | Robust communication about risk across the bank and in reporting to the board and senior management |
| 9 | Compliance | The board oversees compliance risk, establishes a compliance function, and approves compliance policies and processes |
| 10 | Internal audit | Provides independent assurance to the board and supports effective governance and long-term soundness |
| 11 | Compensation | Remuneration supports sound governance and risk management |
| 12 | Disclosure and transparency | Governance is transparent to shareholders, depositors, other stakeholders, and market participants |
| 13 | Role of supervisors | Supervisors guide and evaluate bank governance, interact regularly with boards and senior management, require remedial action, and share information with other supervisors |

### Restructuring and the Dodd-Frank Act

From 1933 until 1999 the **Glass-Steagall Act** separated commercial banking from investment banking in the United States. The **Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act** of 1999 removed most of those restrictions and let bank holding companies become financial holding companies that combine commercial banking, investment banking, insurance, and broker-dealer activities. The two industries still ran under different regimes: commercial banks were subject to conduct supervision and prudential regulation aimed at stability, while investment banks were outside bank regulators' prudential oversight because they were considered marginal to banking stability. The crisis disproved that view. Bear Stearns and Merrill Lynch were merged into banks under duress, Lehman Brothers went bankrupt, and Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley became bank holding companies, which put them under full bank regulation and gave them access to Federal Reserve credit.

The **Dodd-Frank Act**, signed in July 2010 and running to about 2,300 pages, overhauled US financial regulation to improve consumer protection and systemic stability:

| Area | Provision |
|---|---|
| Federal Reserve powers | **Systemically important financial institutions (SIFIs)**, defined as bank holding companies with more than USD 50 billion of assets (raised to USD 250 billion in 2018), are regulated by the Fed, whose mandate now includes macroprudential supervision |
| Too big to fail | An **orderly liquidation authority (OLA)** to wind down failing firms |
| Resolution planning | SIFIs submit a **living will** to the Fed and the FDIC setting out how they could be resolved |
| Volcker Rule | Prohibits proprietary trading by banking entities and limits their ownership of, or partnership in, hedge funds and private equity funds (effective July 2015) |
| Derivatives | A transparency-focused overhaul of derivatives regulation to help market participants manage counterparty risk |
| Consumer protection | A **Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB)** regulates consumer financial products and services |
| Stress testing | A new supervisory approach to scenario analysis and stress testing |

The Dodd-Frank stress testing approach is:

- Top-down, with macroeconomic scenarios that unfold over several quarters.
- Applied to credit, liquidity, market, and operational risk.
- Computationally demanding, because risk drivers are not stationary, and realistic, allowing for active management of portfolios during the scenario.
- Built into each bank's business, capital, and liquidity planning.
- Run across all large banks at once, showing how a common severe scenario would affect them collectively.

The Federal Reserve runs two exercises:

| | DFAST | CCAR |
|---|---|---|
| Full name | Dodd-Frank Act Stress Test | Comprehensive Capital Analysis and Review |
| Banks covered | Assets above USD 10 billion | Assets above USD 50 billion (mandatory only above USD 250 billion after the 2018 reform) |
| Content | Supervisory stress test of capital | Annual; three supervisory scenarios plus two bank-generated scenarios (BHC baseline and BHC adverse); a capital plan covering all planned capital actions, such as dividend increases, share buybacks, and major acquisitions, over nine quarters; a common equity Tier 1 capital ratio of at least 4.5% throughout; a qualitative review of the adequacy of internal processes |

### The European response: SREP and EBA stress tests

Banks supervised under Europe's Single Supervisory Mechanism are assessed through the **Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process (SREP)**, which rests on three principles:

1. A forward-looking focus on whether each bank's business model is sustainable, including under stress.
2. An assessment method based on industry best practice.
3. The expectation that every bank will eventually operate under the same standards.

Its two key components are the **internal capital adequacy assessment process (ICAAP)**, which uses scenario analysis and stress testing to support capital planning, and the **internal liquidity adequacy assessment process (ILAAP)**, which covers losses from selling assets and higher funding costs in stress.

Banks with assets of EUR 30 billion or more must also run **European Banking Authority (EBA) stress tests** at the consolidated group level, excluding insurance activities. The regulator supplies a baseline scenario and an adverse scenario covering three years. Unlike CCAR, the approach is static: banks report the immediate impact of the cumulative three-year shocks.

## The board of directors

### Whose interests the board protects

The board's traditional duty is to protect shareholders. A growing view extends it to all stakeholders, including debtholders and employees, whose interests diverge: debtholders care most about extreme downside risk, because their claim is at stake only when the firm's solvency is in question. The board also oversees executive management, and analyzing the risks and returns of the firm's activities is one of its basic duties: when management takes a risk, the board must understand the type and size of the loss it could cause.

**Agency problems** are conflicts of interest between management and shareholders. They usually appear as risk taken to boost short-term profits or apparent performance at the expense of longer-term stakeholders, and they are easy to create. Stock options, which pay off only if the share price exceeds a set level, can encourage executives to push the price up temporarily even when that harms the firm later. No pay scheme removes the temptation entirely, which is why boards are expected to be independent of management, why executive accountability measures are spreading, and why separating the roles of CEO and board chair is recommended practice.

MF Global shows what happens without that independence. In 2010 Jon Corzine became both chairman and CEO of the brokerage, which already had liquidity and compliance problems. Despite repeated warnings from the CRO, the firm made large proprietary investments in European sovereign debt. They soured in 2011, deepening the liquidity problems, and shareholders and clients lost confidence, which brought the firm down. The firm allegedly misused client funds to stay solvent, and the US Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) took action against Corzine and the firm's assistant treasurer.

### The board's risk responsibilities

Corporate governance and risk management objectives have converged, and post-crisis regulation raised the standard for both. In risk governance, the board:

- **Assesses the risks and rewards in the business strategy**, based on a clear understanding of the firm's direction and goals, and takes an active part in strategic planning.
- **Sets the risk appetite** and matches strategic objectives to it during planning. Some activities are unacceptable because of the type of risk; others are acceptable only up to a size relative to the firm's total assets. Risk must be considered from the start of business planning, and the appetite and current risk position must be communicated clearly so that limits can be set.
- **Ensures oversight and transparency.** Major transactions must fit authorized risk and strategy, and disclosure to managers and stakeholders must be adequate and meet internal rules and regulations. The board is ultimately responsible when risk policy is ignored or violated.
- **Confirms that an effective risk management system exists**, with procedures for identifying, assessing, and handling business, operational, reputational, market, liquidity, compliance, and credit risk. Firms fail through willful excessive risk taking, and just as often through failing to identify or assess risks in time.
- **Aligns compensation with risk-adjusted performance**, so that incentives do not conflict with shareholders' interests.
- **Checks that its information is accurate and reliable**, drawing on the CEO, other senior executives, and internal and external auditors, and builds enough knowledge to ask hard questions and understand the answers.

The board's role is proactive but not day to day: it makes sure the processes for delegating and carrying out risk decisions work as planned. Directors need training on risk and on defining risk appetite, so they can judge the firm's capacity for risk over a given horizon in light of its business mix, earnings goals, strategy, and competitive position. The board should have a risk committee whose members have enough analytical skill and business experience, and that committee should be separate from the audit committee because the two jobs need different skills.

### Four basic choices

However complex the process, managing corporate risk comes down to four choices:

1. Whether to undertake an activity.
2. Whether to transfer all or part of a risk to third parties, for example through insurance or hedging.
3. Whether to mitigate a risk in advance through early detection and prevention.
4. Whether to assume a risk, with full awareness of its upside and downside.

These choices should be made across the whole enterprise under unified policies and methods, which is enterprise risk management, supported by infrastructure (physical resources and defined processes) able to handle that scope.

### Is risk management taken seriously?

The people employed in risk management and their standing in the hierarchy show how seriously a firm takes it:

- Is the risk manager part of the executive team, and can the role lead to other senior positions?
- How independent is the risk manager, what authority does the role carry, and to whom does it report?
- Are risk managers paid well compared with employees rewarded for performance, such as traders?
- Is the firm's ethical culture strong enough to withstand bad actors, and are clear ethical standards actively enforced?

## Risk appetite statement

The Financial Stability Board (FSB) defines a **risk appetite statement (RAS)** as "a written articulation of the aggregate level and types of risk that a firm will accept or avoid in order to achieve its business objectives." It contains both qualitative and quantitative statements and should also cover risks that are hard to quantify, such as reputation and conduct risk, money laundering, and unethical practices.

A bank's RAS objectives typically include safeguarding its reputation and brand, balancing risk and return, keeping a prudent attitude toward tail risk and event risk, meeting regulatory expectations, and achieving or keeping a target credit rating, such as AA, all within a risk capacity defined by regulatory constraints. According to the FSB, an effective RAS should:

- Include the background information and assumptions behind the strategic and business plans at the time they were approved.
- Be linked to the firm's short- and long-term strategic, capital, and financial plans and to its compensation programs.
- Contain risk appetite and risk tolerance measures that limit risk at the business unit level and for the organization as a whole.
- Make clear how risk appetite, risk capacity, risk tolerance, and the current risk profile relate to each other.

Banks also publish, for each risk type, the governing policy, the kinds of limits used, and the committees responsible for oversight: for example, credit concentration limits and delegated approval authorities for credit risk, liquidity and funding limits for liquidity risk, and key risk indicators for operational and reputation risk.

### Capacity, appetite, tolerance, and profile

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Risk capacity** | The maximum risk the firm can bear |
| **Risk appetite** | The aggregate amount of risk the firm is willing to accept, set sufficiently below capacity |
| **Risk tolerance** | The range of acceptable outcomes around a specific business objective; a tactical band, narrower in scope than appetite |
| **Risk profile** | The risk the firm actually holds |

The goal, as COSO's 2017 enterprise risk management framework describes it, is to keep the actual risk profile inside the tolerance bands. Operating inside them gives management confidence that the firm can reach its target risk-adjusted return while limiting the amount at risk.

## Board committees

At most firms, the board's audit and risk committees approve the key risk policies and procedures, review how they are implemented, and judge whether they work. The committees translate the board-approved risk appetite into practical restrictions, which executives and department heads then spread through the organization.

### The audit committee

The **audit committee** is accountable for the accuracy and completeness of the firm's financial and regulatory disclosures, and for compliance with best practice in non-financial matters: regulatory, legal, compliance, and risk management activities all fall within its remit. Its distinctive job is **independent verification** that the firm is doing what it says it is doing.

The committee judges quality as well as accuracy. Reviewing the financial statements, it confirms that they are accurate and that the firm adequately addresses the risk of material misstatement. Before 2007–2009, many audit committees failed to uncover excessive risk in proprietary trading or to warn their boards about disproportionately large positions in structured credit products.

Management prepares the financial statements; the audit committee checks that the process behind them is controlled and produces accurate results.

Members must be knowledgeable, financially literate, independent in judgment, and of the highest integrity, and must be willing to challenge management. At most banks a director who is not an executive chairs the committee and most members are independent. The relationship with management can be adversarial, but lines of communication must stay open.

### The board risk management committee

The **board risk management committee** sets the firm's risk appetite and independently reviews the governance of all material risks, including policy guidelines, methodologies, and infrastructure. The board delegates to it the approval and review of risk levels, under formally approved and documented terms. The committee monitors financial, operational, business, reputational, and strategic risks, keeps direct contact with internal and external auditors, and reports to the board on matters such as extending special credit when the firm would exceed board-set limits.

### Risk advisory directors

Independent directors are often recruited from outside financial services by design, and executives can easily mislead or overwhelm directors who lack the skills or confidence to challenge them. Training programs and outside professional support help. Another answer is a **risk advisory director**: an independent board member, not necessarily a voting one, who specializes in risk. This director strengthens the audit committee and the executive risk committee by examining risk policies, methods, and infrastructure, keeps the board informed of best practice, and gives a professional view of the risks in the core business model and in new activities.

## From risk appetite to limits

### How limits flow down

1. The **board risk committee** approves the risk appetite each year, based on broad but clearly defined metrics, such as the bank's total interest rate risk.
2. The **senior risk committee**, led by the CEO and typically including the CRO, CFO, treasurer, chief compliance officer, and business unit heads, is empowered by the board to implement and oversee the risk appetite framework. It sets limiting parameters for financial risks (credit, market) and non-financial risks (business, operational), often through sub-committees such as a credit risk committee, and recommends to the board risk committee the total risk it considers prudent.
3. The senior risk committee establishes, documents, and enforces risk policies, sets limits for specific business activities, and passes them to the CRO. It delegates day-to-day decisions to the CRO, including approving risks above a business's preset limits, provided they stay within the overall board-approved limits.
4. Each business unit receives a mandate to take risk up to a ceiling. The senior risk committee reviews and approves each mandate periodically, usually for one year, and the CRO monitors the limits and may extend a mandate briefly to fit the committee's schedule.

**Business risk committees**, made up of business and risk staff, exist for each major business line. They align business decisions with the firm's risk-reward targets, set out how each risk is handled in the business, approve business-specific risk policies, and review business-level limits in detail.

### The chief risk officer

The CRO is part of management but needs real authority and independence, as the 2007–2009 crisis and MF Global showed. Best practice is that the CRO:

- Designs the risk management program and is responsible for risk policies, methodologies, infrastructure, and governance inside the firm.
- Takes part in setting risk strategy as well as implementing and overseeing it.
- Reports directly to the CEO and has a seat on the board risk committee.
- Has a voice in approving new financial instruments and new business lines.
- Has a clear mandate to raise anything that could compromise the risk appetite or risk policy with management at every level and with the board.
- Acts as liaison between the board and management, keeping the board informed of weaknesses, and can order units to cut back or close positions.

### Designing limits

Limits are needed for each business, for the specific risks within it, and for the firm's whole portfolio. **Market risk limits** constrain exposure to changes in prices, interest rates, and exchange rates. **Credit risk limits** cap exposure to defaults and to deterioration in credit quality, from lending and from derivatives. Banks also set limits for asset-liability management, liquidity, and even catastrophe risk. A limit's form depends on the risk, the firm's competitive position, and the range of its activities, and the processes for setting limits, reviewing exposures, approving exceptions, and analyzing methods should all be documented.

VaR suits typical portfolios in fairly normal markets but works less well in stress or for specialized portfolios, so best practice builds scenario analysis and stress testing into the limit framework to confirm the firm would survive worst-case conditions. Banks usually set two kinds of limit:

| | Tier 1 limits | Tier 2 limits |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Specific | More general |
| Examples | Overall limit by asset class; overall stress-test limit; maximum drawdown limit | Limits by business activity; aggregated exposures by credit rating, industry, maturity, and region |
| When exceeded | Must be cleared or corrected immediately | Less urgent; cleared within a few days or a week |

The CRO proposes the standards for limit metrics and the internal risk committee approves them. Limits should rarely be exceeded in normal business, so they must reflect each unit's historical behavior and leave a margin for error. In one illustrative design, normal exposures run at 40% to 60% of a Tier 1 market risk limit and peak use under normal market conditions reaches 75% to 85%. When all limits are computed on a common basis, such as economic capital, Tier 2 limits can be applied across business lines.

:::example Setting and monitoring a Tier 1 limit
A trading desk has a Tier 1 market risk limit of USD 20 million, designed with the illustrative ranges above.

1. Normal exposure: 40% to 60% of the limit, or USD 8 million to USD 12 million.
2. Peak use in normal markets: 75% to 85%, or USD 15 million to USD 17 million.
3. If the bank requires an alert at 85% of the limit, the desk head notifies risk management when exposure reaches USD 17 million. The CRO and the desk head can then ask the business risk committee for a temporary increase, which goes to the senior risk committee for final approval.
:::

### Monitoring limits and handling exceptions

Setting limits starts the process rather than ending it. Market risk is the most time-sensitive risk and needs continual monitoring:

- Positions are valued daily, and profit and loss statements are prepared outside the trading department and sent to executives who are not traders.
- The assumptions in valuation models are verified independently.
- The desk's compliance with policy and limits, and the escalation of exceptions, are documented promptly, and written procedures separate acceptable exceptions from unacceptable violations.
- Actual portfolio volatility is compared regularly with the volatility the risk model predicted, and stress tests measure the effect of large market and credit moves on earnings.
- Intraday trading exposure may have to come from the front office's accumulated client orders, but data for monitoring market risk limits should come from consolidated market data feeds independent of front-office systems, reconciled with the bank's official books.

Business units must warn risk management before an expected limit breach, and a planned, pre-announced excess is more likely to be approved. When a limit is breached, risk management records it immediately on a **daily limit exception report** that separates Tier 1 from Tier 2 exceptions and states the circumstances, the reason, and the plan for dealing with it. The CRO lists all exceptions on an **enterprise exception report** for the daily risk meeting. No manager, including the CEO, may remove an exceedance from the daily exception report.

Limits have an opportunity cost: a bank that refuses additional risk forgoes some profit. As a unit nears a limit, the bank should weigh costs, benefits, and risks before granting an exception.

## Incentives and risk taking

Before the crisis, bonuses at many financial institutions were tied to short-term profits or to business volume, which encouraged staff to take income early and push risk into the future. The pay was shaped like a **call option**: large rewards when the bank made money and no penalty when it lost. Combined with high leverage, it let staff effectively bet the bank.

:::example Why option-like pay encourages risk
A trader receives 10% of any positive annual profit and nothing if the desk loses money.

| Strategy | Outcomes | Expected profit to the bank | Expected bonus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Risky | +USD 50m or −USD 50m, each with probability 50% | 0 | \(0.5 \times 5 + 0.5 \times 0 =\) USD 2.5m |
| Safe | +USD 10m with certainty | USD 10m | USD 1.0m |

The trader earns more from the strategy the bank should reject. If 10% of any loss were clawed back from deferred pay, the risky strategy's expected pay would fall to \(0.5 \times 5 + 0.5 \times (-5) = 0\), and the safe strategy would pay the trader more.
:::

Many jurisdictions now require public firms to have a board **compensation committee** set executive pay, because CEOs could otherwise persuade directors to overpay executives at shareholders' expense. Compensation is treated as part of risk culture: it should align with the long-term interests of shareholders and other stakeholders and with risk-adjusted return on capital, rewarding calculated rather than reckless risk. Building risk measures into performance targets is leading practice, and compensation planning is increasingly part of enterprise risk management. Firms remain tempted to overpay "rainmakers" who generate unusual revenue, and without international cooperation, banks can move activity to jurisdictions with looser pay rules.

In September 2009 the G-20 endorsed the FSB's compensation standards:

- Eliminate multi-year guaranteed bonuses.
- Expose executives to downside through deferred compensation, share-based pay that rewards long-term value, and **clawbacks** that recover bonuses if losses emerge later.
- Limit variable compensation relative to total net revenues.
- Require disclosure to improve transparency.
- Keep the committees that oversee pay independent, so that pay stays aligned with performance and risk.

In 2014 the FSB reported that these standards were essentially implemented. The European Union went further and capped bonuses at 100% of salary, or 200% with the approval of two-thirds of shareholders.

:::example EU bonus cap
A banker in the EU earns a fixed salary of EUR 800,000.

1. Maximum variable pay under the standard cap: \(1.00 \times 800{,}000 =\) EUR 800,000.
2. With approval from two-thirds of shareholders: \(2.00 \times 800{,}000 =\) EUR 1.6 million.
:::

Share-based pay aligns executives with shareholders only partly. Lehman Brothers employees owned about a third of the firm's shares before it collapsed, and because shareholders keep all the upside while losing at most their investment, share ownership can itself encourage risk. One remedy is to make employees creditors. In 2013 UBS paid part of its highest earners' bonuses in **bonus bonds** that are forfeited if the bank's regulatory capital ratio falls below 7.5% or it needs a bailout; in 2012 the European Banking Authority had suggested that senior bankers receive part of their bonuses in bonds that lose value in a crisis.

## How organizational units share risk management

Board committees oversee risk, but staff carry out risk management at almost every level. Executives and business line managers manage, monitor, and report risk together:

| Unit | Risk management responsibilities |
|---|---|
| **Senior management** | Sets business-level risk tolerances; designs and manages policy; evaluates performance |
| **Business line** | Takes on and manages exposure to approved risks; verifies that deal capture is timely, accurate, and complete, and affirms the official profit and loss |
| **Risk management** | Develops and implements risk policy; monitors limits; controls model implementation risk; gives senior management independent risk assessments |
| **Finance** | Sets valuation and finance policies; ensures reported earnings are accurate and complete; reviews independent valuation methods and processes; manages business planning and supports business lines' financial needs |
| **Operations** | Executes, records, and settles trades independently; reconciles front-office and back-office positions; documents all transactions; prepares earnings reports and independent valuations, such as marking positions to market |

## The internal audit function

Risk governance cannot confirm on its own that the firm complies with board policy and external regulation. The **internal audit** function provides that assurance by checking how risk management and governance are set up, how they are implemented, and whether they work. Regulators require internal audit to review all processes, policies, and procedures related to risk management, including the organization and documentation of the risk control unit, the integrity of risk governance, and whether risk measures are built into daily business management. Internal auditors:

- Review monitoring procedures and track upgrades to risk management systems.
- Assess the adequacy of controls over how data are generated and secured.
- Confirm that model vetting processes work.
- Compare compliance documentation with the qualitative and quantitative criteria in regulatory guidelines.
- Give an opinion on the reliability of the VaR reporting framework.

For market risk, auditors review the vetting of the derivative valuation models used by the front and back offices, sign off on significant changes to how risk is quantified, confirm the range of risks the models capture, and check the reliability of information systems and the completeness of the data behind market risk metrics. Beyond regulatory requirements, they evaluate the design and conceptual soundness of risk measurement: they validate market risk models by backtesting, review risk management information systems (coding, internal model applications, controls over position data capture), test assumptions about volatility, correlation, and other parameters, and verify the market databases used to generate VaR parameters.

A risk management function can be rated, internally or by outside parties such as rating agencies, to allow comparison across units and firms. The Institute of Internal Auditors' **International Professional Practices Framework (IPPF)** contains mandatory standards and an ethics code that define professional practice, and recommended guidance on how to apply them.

Whether internal audit should oversee operational risk management is debated. Audit has a natural interest in internal controls, but the implementation of risk management must stay separate from auditing. Auditors must be independent of the activity they audit; otherwise conflicts of interest compromise both risk management and audit and weaken the assurance the board relies on.

## Practice questions

:::question
Under the Sarbanes-Oxley Act, who must certify that a public company's financial reports filed with the SEC are accurate and complete?
- [ ] The chief risk officer only
- [ ] The chief executive officer only
- [x] The chief executive officer and the chief financial officer
- [ ] The chief executive officer, the chief financial officer, and the chief risk officer
---
SOX requires the CEO and CFO to certify that filed reports contain no untrue statement or omission of a material fact and fairly present the company's financial condition, and it makes them responsible for internal controls. The CRO has no certification duty under SOX.
:::

:::question
Which Basel III measure is a liquidity standard rather than part of the macroprudential overlay?
- [x] The net stable funding ratio
- [ ] The 3% leverage ratio
- [ ] The countercyclical capital buffer
- [ ] Total loss-absorbing capacity standards for global systemically important banks
---
Basel III's liquidity standards are the 30-day liquidity coverage ratio and the one-year net stable funding ratio. The macroprudential overlay consists of the leverage ratio, the countercyclical buffer, TLAC for G-SIBs, rules for systemically important markets and infrastructures such as central clearing, and capturing systemic and tail risk in models and stress tests.
:::

:::question
Which of the following was not introduced by the Dodd-Frank Act?
- [x] A requirement for the CEO and CFO to certify the accuracy of financial statements
- [ ] Living wills for systemically important financial institutions
- [ ] A prohibition on proprietary trading by banking entities
- [ ] The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau
---
CEO and CFO certification of financial statements comes from the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002. Dodd-Frank (2010) introduced living wills, the Volcker Rule, the CFPB, the orderly liquidation authority, derivatives reform, Fed supervision of SIFIs, and a new stress testing regime.
:::

:::question
How does the European Banking Authority stress test differ from the Federal Reserve's CCAR?
- [x] The EBA test is static, measuring the immediate impact of cumulative shocks from three-year scenarios, while CCAR requires a capital plan covering planned capital actions over nine quarters
- [ ] The EBA test uses only bank-generated scenarios, while CCAR uses only supervisory scenarios
- [ ] The EBA test applies to banks with assets above USD 10 billion, while CCAR applies to all banks
- [ ] The EBA test includes insurance activities, while CCAR excludes them
---
EBA tests give banks with at least EUR 30 billion of assets a baseline and an adverse scenario over three years, applied at group level excluding insurance, and ask for the immediate impact of the cumulative shocks. CCAR combines three supervisory scenarios with two bank-generated ones, requires a nine-quarter capital plan covering dividends, buybacks, and acquisitions, and includes a qualitative review of internal processes.
:::

:::question
A desk's Tier 1 market risk limit is USD 25 million. The bank designs limits so that normal exposures use 40% to 60% of the limit and peak use under normal conditions reaches 75% to 85%. Which exposure pattern fits this design?
- [ ] Normal exposure of USD 20–25 million, with peaks at the limit
- [x] Normal exposure of USD 10–15 million, with peaks of about USD 19–21 million
- [ ] Normal exposure of USD 2.5–5 million, with peaks of about USD 10 million
- [ ] Normal exposure just below USD 25 million, since unused limit is wasted capacity
---
40% to 60% of USD 25 million is USD 10–15 million, and 75% to 85% is USD 18.75–21.25 million. Limits are designed to be exceeded rarely in normal business, so exposure should not run close to the limit; the gap absorbs volatility and error.
:::

:::question
Which statement about limit exceptions reflects best practice?
- [ ] Tier 2 exceedances must be corrected immediately, while Tier 1 exceedances can wait up to a week
- [x] Tier 1 exceedances must be cleared or corrected immediately, and no manager, including the CEO, may remove an exceedance from the daily exception report
- [ ] The CEO may leave a Tier 1 exceedance off the exception report if the position is expected to be profitable
- [ ] An exceedance needs to be reported only if it was not approved in advance
---
Tier 1 limits are specific (by asset class, stress test, maximum drawdown), and their breaches must be fixed immediately; Tier 2 breaches can be cleared within a few days or a week. Every breach goes on the daily limit exception report and the enterprise exception report, and nobody may exclude one. Advance notice makes approval of a planned excess more likely but does not remove the reporting requirement.
:::

:::question
A trader receives 20% of any positive annual profit and nothing when the desk loses money. Strategy X produces a profit of USD 40 million or a loss of USD 40 million with equal probability. Strategy Y produces a certain profit of USD 8 million. What is the trader's expected bonus under each strategy?
- [x] X: USD 4.0 million; Y: USD 1.6 million
- [ ] X: USD 0; Y: USD 1.6 million
- [ ] X: USD 8.0 million; Y: USD 1.6 million
- [ ] X: USD 4.0 million; Y: USD 8.0 million
---
X: \(0.5 \times (0.20 \times 40) + 0.5 \times 0 = 4.0\). Y: \(0.20 \times 8 = 1.6\). The trader prefers X even though the bank expects a profit of 0 under X and USD 8 million under Y, because the bonus is shaped like a call option on profits.

A bonus of 0 under X would require losses to reduce pay symmetrically. USD 8.0 million under X ignores the 50% chance of a loss. USD 8.0 million under Y is the bank's profit, not the trader's bonus.
:::

:::question
Which of the following is not a responsibility of the board audit committee?
- [ ] Assessing the quality, not only the accuracy, of financial reporting and internal controls
- [x] Preparing the annual financial statements
- [ ] Overseeing compliance with regulatory, legal, and risk management standards
- [ ] Providing independent verification that the firm is doing what it claims to do
---
Management prepares the financial statements. The audit committee checks that the process producing them is controlled and accurate, judges the quality of reporting, controls, compliance, and risk management, and gives the board independent verification.
:::

:::question
Which arrangement for a bank's chief risk officer best reflects post-crisis best practice?
- [ ] The CRO reports to the head of trading, whose desks generate most of the bank's market risk
- [x] The CRO reports directly to the CEO, sits on the board risk committee, and can take threats to the risk appetite to the board
- [ ] The CRO reports to the head of internal audit, who also implements operational risk management
- [ ] The CRO advises the senior risk committee but cannot require business units to reduce positions
---
CRO independence requires a direct line to the CEO, a seat on the board risk committee, a voice in approving new products and businesses, authority to order position cuts, and a mandate to escalate issues to the board. Reporting to a business head creates a conflict of interest, and internal audit must stay independent of the risk management it reviews.
:::

:::question
How does risk tolerance differ from risk appetite?
- [ ] Risk tolerance is the maximum risk the firm can bear, while risk appetite is the risk it currently holds
- [x] Risk tolerance is a tactical range of acceptable outcomes around a specific business objective, while risk appetite is the broader aggregate amount of risk the firm will accept
- [ ] Risk tolerance is set by regulators, while risk appetite is set by the trading desks
- [ ] Risk tolerance applies only to non-financial risks
---
Risk capacity is the maximum risk the firm can bear, risk appetite is the aggregate amount it will accept (set below capacity), risk tolerance is the band of acceptable outcomes for a particular objective, and the risk profile is what the firm holds now. Management aims to keep the risk profile inside the tolerance bands.
:::

:::question
In 2013 UBS paid part of its highest earners' bonuses in bonds that are forfeited if its regulatory capital ratio falls below 7.5% or it needs a bailout. Which problem does this address?
- [ ] Share-based pay gives employees too little benefit from the bank's upside
- [x] Share ownership can still encourage risk taking, because shareholders keep unlimited upside with limited downside; bonus bonds give employees a creditor's interest in avoiding failure
- [ ] Deferred pay makes it hard to recruit staff who generate revenue
- [ ] The EU bonus cap prevents paying more than 100% of salary in any form
---
Employees paid in shares gain from risky bets that succeed and lose only their stake when bets fail; Lehman Brothers staff owned about a third of their firm before it collapsed. Bonds that are wiped out when capital runs low tie pay to the bank's survival, the outcome creditors care about.
:::

:::question
Why should internal audit not be responsible for implementing operational risk management?
- [ ] Internal auditors have no interest in the quality of internal controls
- [x] Auditors must be independent of the activities they audit, or conflicts of interest compromise both risk management and the assurance given to the board
- [ ] Regulators prohibit internal audit from reviewing risk management processes
- [ ] Operational risk cannot be audited because it cannot be measured
---
Internal audit must review risk management processes and has a natural interest in internal controls, but it must stay separate from the implementation it assesses. An auditor reviewing its own work cannot give the board credible independent assurance.
:::

:::question
What governance weakness does the collapse of MF Global in 2011 best illustrate?
- [x] A combined chairman and CEO who overrode the CRO's warnings about large proprietary positions
- [ ] Too much reliance on a board risk advisory director
- [ ] A risk appetite statement that was too conservative
- [ ] Keeping the audit committee separate from the risk committee
---
Jon Corzine was both chairman and CEO, and the firm made large proprietary bets on European sovereign debt despite its CRO's warnings. When the bets soured, liquidity problems and lost confidence brought the firm down. The case supports separating the CEO and chair roles, keeping the board independent of management, and giving the CRO real authority.
:::
