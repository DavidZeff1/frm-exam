## Why banks transfer credit risk

Credit risk is a bank's core exposure. A traditional bank funds long-term, illiquid loans with short-term deposits and holds the loans until they mature, and until the end of the 1990s banks had little ability to manage that exposure except by lending less. In 2002 Federal Reserve Chairman Alan Greenspan described a "new paradigm of active credit management," arguing that US banks had weathered the 2001–2002 slowdown partly because credit default swaps, collateralized debt obligations, and collateralized loan obligations had transferred and dispersed their credit exposures.

The 2007–2009 crisis damaged the reputation of these instruments, but they were not the main cause. The failures lay in how the pre-crisis securitization process was run and in how firms used and abused the products, including conflicts of interest and a lack of transparency. Markets where risks stayed transparent kept working:

| Fate after the crisis | Instruments |
|---|---|
| Kept functioning or recovered | Credit default swaps (single-name and index); asset-backed securities (ABS) backed by auto loans, credit card receivables, equipment leases, and student loans; collateralized loan obligations (CLOs), whose issuance grew strongly from 2011 and surpassed pre-crisis volumes |
| Expected to return and draw renewed interest | Asset-backed commercial paper (ABCP); privately issued mortgage-backed securities (MBS) |
| Unlikely to return | CDO-squared, single-tranche CDOs, and complex ABCP, whose complexity was designed to make them easier to market rather than better at hedging |

New forms of credit risk transfer keep appearing, such as insurance companies buying bank loans to build asset portfolios that match their long-term liabilities. Post-crisis capital rules make holding every loan to maturity an inefficient way for banks to manage lending risk, so regulators and banks both want securitization markets that help banks fund themselves and manage risk.

### Key instruments

| Instrument | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Credit derivative** | A contract that transfers credit risk from one party to another |
| **Credit default swap (CDS)** | The most common credit derivative. The protection buyer pays a fee; the protection seller makes a payment if a credit event affects the reference entity or reference obligation. A single-name CDS has one reference entity; a basket CDS has several |
| **Asset-backed security (ABS)** | A security backed by a pool of loans or receivables, such as credit card, student loan, or auto loan ABS |
| **Mortgage-backed security (MBS)** | A security backed by a pool of residential mortgages, issued with government backing (for example through Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac) or by private entities, including subprime MBS |
| **Commercial mortgage-backed security (CMBS)** | A security backed by a pool of commercial mortgage loans |
| **Collateralized debt obligation (CDO)** | A security backed by a pool of debt instruments, such as bonds |
| **CDO-squared** | A security backed by tranches of other CDOs |
| **Collateralized loan obligation (CLO)** | A security backed by a pool of commercial bank loans |
| **Asset-backed commercial paper (ABCP)** | Commercial paper issued by a special purpose vehicle to finance a pool of longer-term receivables |
| **Structured investment vehicle (SIV)** | A pool of assets that tries to earn the spread between short-term funding rates and the yield on long-term structured products such as ABS and MBS |

## Traditional ways to reduce credit exposure

Banks have long used several techniques to limit credit risk to individual names and across portfolios.

**Third-party guarantees.** A lender or investor can buy insurance against default. On an individual obligor, this is a **financial guarantee**, provided by a specialist insurer called a **monoline insurer**. Guarantees were common in the US municipal bond market and in ABS. A monoline could raise a bond to a AAA rating only if the monoline itself was rated AAA. Before the crisis, monolines had insured large amounts of ABS and privately issued MBS. When those securities defaulted, the monolines had to pay out, were downgraded, and in some cases failed, and far fewer municipal bonds and ABS have been issued with guarantees since.

**Netting.** Documentation that allows the amounts owed between two counterparties to be offset makes the exposure the net amount. Without it, if a counterparty goes bankrupt, the bank must still pay everything it owes, while the counterparty's obligation to the bank becomes a claim on the bankrupt estate.

:::example Loss on default with and without netting
A bank owes a counterparty USD 30 million on some trades, and the counterparty owes the bank USD 50 million on others. The counterparty goes bankrupt, and unsecured claims recover 40%.

1. Without enforceable netting, the bank pays the full USD 30 million it owes and recovers \(0.40 \times 50 = 20\) million on its claim. It loses \(50 - 20 = 30\) million.
2. With netting, the positions offset to a net claim of \(50 - 30 = 20\) million. The bank recovers \(0.40 \times 20 = 8\) million and loses \(20 - 8 = 12\) million.
:::

**Marking to market and margining.** Counterparties agree to revalue positions periodically and pay each other any net change in value, which keeps net exposure small. It needs fairly sophisticated systems and was historically typical of exchange-traded derivatives.

**Collateral.** Collateral offsets the loss if a borrower defaults. It is weaker when the event that causes the default also lowers the collateral's value, which is **wrong-way risk**: an oil company that posts barrels of crude as collateral becomes more likely to default exactly when the oil price, and so the collateral, falls.

**Termination triggers and put options.** Counterparties agree at inception that certain events, such as a downgrade or a breach of a balance sheet or income statement metric, require the position to be unwound under a set method, often at the mid-market value. A put option gives the lender the right to force early termination at a set price.

**Reassignment.** A contract can require that the credit exposure be reassigned to another party if a trigger, such as a rating downgrade, occurs.

**Syndication and loan sales.** For very large loans, a lead bank originates the transaction and distributes it among a group of other investors for a percentage fee, usually keeping about 20% of the loan for its own book. Banks can also sell loans they originate or own in the secondary market.

| Syndication type | Who bears a shortfall if investors take less than the target |
|---|---|
| **Firm commitment** | The bank: it guarantees the borrower the full amount and takes any unsold portion onto its own books |
| **Best efforts** | The borrower: the amount raised depends on investor demand, with no guarantee the target is reached |

Syndicated loans are the backbone of the secondary market for bank loans, because the arranging bank must make sure investors can trade the loan after distribution. As that market and the credit derivatives market grew, loan pricing became more transparent and liquidity increased.

These techniques are effective, but each needs a specific agreement between the counterparties, which may not suit their needs. More importantly, none of them separates credit risk from the underlying position so it can be sold to a wide range of investors, and none lets a bank slice credit risk into pieces to fine-tune a position or portfolio.

## Credit derivatives

A **credit derivative** is an off-balance-sheet contract that transfers the credit risk of a reference asset between two counterparties without selling the asset. Credit derivatives do for credit risk what interest rate and foreign exchange derivatives did for market risk: they isolate the risk from funding and liquidity. A bank can keep a loan, keep serving the borrower, and still pass on the default risk, with no funding or client relationship consequences.

In a CDS, the **protection buyer** pays a periodic premium, quoted as a spread in basis points per year on the notional amount. If a **credit event**, such as a default, affects the reference entity, the **protection seller** compensates the buyer for the loss on the reference obligation, either with a cash payment or by buying the defaulted debt at face value.

:::formula Credit default swap cash flows
$$\text{Annual premium} = s \times N \qquad \text{Payment after a credit event} = (1 - R) \times N$$
- \(s\): CDS spread, per year, in decimal form
- \(N\): notional amount
- \(R\): recovery rate on the reference obligation
:::

:::example Hedging a loan with a CDS
A bank has lent USD 10 million to a company and buys five-year CDS protection on USD 10 million of notional at a spread of 150 basis points.

1. Annual premium: \(0.0150 \times 10{,}000{,}000 = 150{,}000\), paid in quarterly installments of USD 37,500.
2. If the company defaults and its debt recovers 35%, the protection seller pays \((1 - 0.35) \times 10{,}000{,}000 = 6{,}500{,}000\), offsetting the bank's loss on the loan.
3. The bank now has credit exposure to the protection seller instead of the borrower; it loses only if the seller fails as well.
:::

Credit derivatives bring their own challenges:

- **Understanding the contract.** Each counterparty must understand how much risk is transferred and of what kind, how trigger events are defined, what periodic payments are owed, what each side's rights and obligations are when a trigger occurs, and when the contract can or cannot be enforced.
- **Counterparty and systemic concentration risk.** Protection is only as good as the seller. Even before the crisis, regulators worried that a small number of dealers provided most of the liquidity in credit derivatives and that distress at one or several of them could disrupt the market. The single-name and index CDS markets nonetheless operated relatively smoothly at the height of the crisis, under the leadership of the International Swaps and Derivatives Association (ISDA).

### Price discovery and credit spread risk

In a robust, liquid, transparent market, credit derivatives help discover the market price of a given credit risk. CDS spreads let risk managers monitor default risk continuously, while credit ratings are reassessed only periodically. Corporate bond markets also reveal credit risk, but bond yields blend interest rate, credit, and sometimes liquidity risk, and only large companies issue bonds actively enough to support analysis. Credit derivatives can help price the credit risk in privately traded high-yield loans and loan portfolios.

In a mature credit market, credit risk includes **credit spread risk** as well as default risk. The **credit spread** is the yield on an instrument with credit risk minus the yield on a Treasury of the same maturity. When spreads widen, every instrument exposed to that credit loses value even if no one defaults, so for liquid credit assets, credit risk becomes the market risk of credit risk.

:::formula Credit spread and the price effect of a spread change
$$\text{Spread} = y_{\text{credit}} - y_{\text{Treasury}} \qquad \frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{\text{mod}} \times \Delta\text{Spread}$$
- \(y\): yields to maturity on instruments of the same maturity
- \(D_{\text{mod}}\): modified duration of the credit instrument, developed in [Book 4, Chapter 12](#/b4-12)
:::

:::example Loss from spread widening without a default
A corporate bond with a modified duration of 6 yields 5.8% while the Treasury of the same maturity yields 4.3%.

1. Spread: \(5.8\% - 4.3\% = 1.5\%\), or 150 basis points.
2. The spread widens to 250 basis points and Treasury yields do not move, so \(\Delta\text{Spread} = 1.0\%\).
3. Price change \(\approx -6 \times 0.010 = -6.0\%\). The holder loses 6% although the issuer has not missed a payment.
:::

## Securitization

**Securitization** repackages loans or other assets into new securities that can be sold in the securities markets. The pool of loans is the collateral for the new securities, and the securities perform only as well as the collateral does. Securitization is a funding vehicle for financial institutions and non-financial companies, which matters because banks' own capital is too small to meet the credit needs of businesses, consumers, and governments. It is also a risk management tool. Banks have securitized mortgages (creating MBS), corporate loans (creating CLOs), and credit card receivables and auto loans (creating ABS).

### Buy-and-hold versus originate-to-distribute

| | Buy-and-hold (originate-to-hold) | Originate-to-distribute (OTD) |
|---|---|---|
| What happens to the loan | The originator keeps it in its portfolio as an investment | The originator pools similar loans and uses the pool as collateral for new securities sold to investors |
| Credit risk to the originator | Retained | Removed, because the originator no longer owns the loans |
| Price risk to the originator | Retained | Removed |
| Liquidity risk to the originator | Retained, because the loans are illiquid | Removed, because the illiquid loans have been turned into cash |

### The special purpose vehicle

The key element of a securitization is a **special purpose vehicle (SPV)**, a legal entity established by the originating firm, which is called the **sponsor**.

1. The SPV buys the pool of loans from the sponsor and takes ownership of them.
2. It raises the money to pay for the pool by selling the new securities.
3. Holders of the securities receive interest and principal according to set rules for distributing the pool's cash flows and for allocating defaults, known as the **waterfall**.

The securities are issued in classes called **tranches**:

- **Senior bonds** have the most protection against credit losses and are typically rated AAA.
- **Junior bonds**, possibly in several classes, carry ratings below AAA.
- The **equity** or residual tranche receives cash only after all debt classes are paid, so it absorbs losses first and carries the most credit risk. Equity usually provides less than 10% of the funding.

The SPV, not the originator, issues the securities, and the SPV does not guarantee them: investors are paid only from the collateral.

:::formula Loss allocated to a tranche
$$\text{Tranche loss as a fraction of the tranche}=\frac{\min\!\big(\max(L-a,\ 0),\ d-a\big)}{d-a}$$
- \(L\): pool loss as a fraction of the pool's principal
- \(a\): attachment point, the pool loss at which the tranche starts to lose money
- \(d\): detachment point, the pool loss at which the tranche is wiped out
:::

:::example Losses flowing through the tranches
A USD 100 million loan pool backs an SPV that issues an equity tranche absorbing pool losses from 0% to 5% (USD 5 million), a junior tranche from 5% to 20% (USD 15 million), and a senior tranche from 20% to 100% (USD 80 million).

| Pool loss | Equity loss | Junior loss | Senior loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| USD 3m (3%) | USD 3m (60%) | 0 | 0 |
| USD 7m (7%) | USD 5m (100%) | USD 2m (13.3%) | 0 |
| USD 25m (25%) | USD 5m (100%) | USD 15m (100%) | USD 5m (6.25%) |

For the 7% pool loss, the junior tranche loses \(\min(\max(0.07 - 0.05,\ 0),\ 0.15)/0.15 = 0.02/0.15 = 13.3\%\) of its principal.
:::

Non-banks use securitization to raise funds and manage risk:

- **General Motors** lends to car buyers through its captive finance company, GM Financial, whose SPVs, such as AmeriCredit Automobile Receivables Trust, buy and securitize its auto loans and leases.
- **Harley-Davidson Financial Services** lends to buyers of the company's motorcycles; its SPV issued its first securitization in 2016.
- **SoFi**, a personal finance company, issued USD 2.6 billion of student loan and consumer loan securitizations in April 2018.
- **Sprint** has securitized receivables from its wireless customers.

Modern securitization began in 1968 with the creation of the Government National Mortgage Association (Ginnie Mae), the main channel for securitizing government-insured mortgages. Consumer ABS in the United States and residential MBS in the United Kingdom emerged in the 1980s, US commercial MBS in the 1990s, and between 2000 and 2007 the US private-label market saw a surge of very complex, risky, and opaque CDOs. According to the International Monetary Fund, securitization issuance grew from almost nothing in the early 1990s to almost USD 5 trillion in 2006, then collapsed after the subprime crisis; only securities backed by credit card receivables, auto loans, and leases were relatively unaffected.

## Originate-to-distribute and the crisis

From the 1980s, banks shifted from buy-and-hold toward the OTD model, selling credit risk and the associated cash flows to investors through ABS and similar products. Basel capital rules drove part of the shift, since moving capital-consuming loans off the balance sheet freed capital, and accounting and regulatory standards encouraged banks to focus on the upfront fees securitization produces.

The model offered each party something:

- **Originators** gained capital efficiency, more funding opportunities, and lower earnings volatility, at least in the short term, because credit risk and interest rate risk appeared to be spread across many market participants.
- **Investors** gained a wider range of investments with which to diversify and to match risk and return to their goals.
- **Borrowers** gained more available credit, more product options, and lower borrowing costs.

The benefits eroded as risks accumulated before the crisis. There is disagreement about how much OTD contributed to the crisis, but broad consensus that it created **moral hazard**: a lender that expects to sell a loan has less reason to maintain underwriting standards or to monitor the borrower, and too few safeguards offset that weakened incentive.

### How banks departed from the model

Before the crisis, many banks did not act as pure intermediaries transferring risk from mortgage lenders to capital market investors. They acted as investors. Relatively little mortgage credit risk actually left the banking system: many banks kept, or even bought, large amounts of securitized mortgage risk. According to a 2008 estimate, half of AA-rated ABS were held by banks (about 30%, largely passed between banks) and by ABCP conduits and SIVs (about 20%).

Risk that should have been widely dispersed was concentrated in highly leveraged, off-balance-sheet **ABCP conduits and SIVs**, set up largely to avoid capital requirements. Some SIVs had large liquidity and maturity mismatches, funding long-term structured products with short-term paper, which left them open to a classic run. Banks misjudged the risks in their commitments to these vehicles' investors, including reputation risk, and falsely assumed that funding would remain available and that markets for the assets would stay liquid enough to keep securitizing. Firms that intended to sell their credit exposures also found themselves holding a growing **pipeline** of loans awaiting securitization, without measuring what would happen if those assets could not be sold.

Bank leverage, poor underwriting, and many firms' decision to retain rather than transfer the credit risk in the securities they created made these weaknesses worse. The problems to fix:

- **Misaligned incentives** along the securitization chain, among originators, organizers, managers, and distributors, driven by short-term profits. Investor oversight weakened as market growth bred complacency, and the products' complexity and investors' limited understanding of them undermined market discipline.
- **Lack of transparency.** Investors had difficulty assessing the quality of the underlying assets and the correlations among them.
- **Poor securitization risk management**, particularly in identifying, assessing, and stress testing market, liquidity, concentration, and pipeline risks.
- **Overreliance on credit ratings.** Rating agencies did not adequately review the detailed data behind securitized transactions and underestimated the risks of subprime CDO structures.

The chain reinforced itself and amplified systemic risk by allowing huge leverage and concentration of risk in the financial sector:

| Link | What went wrong |
|---|---|
| 1. Loan origination | Pay was tied to high loan volumes and high-commission mortgages, not to how loans performed or whether they suited the borrower |
| 2. Securitization | Issuance of high-fee, complex, opaque products soared, requiring advanced financial engineering and large quantities of underlying loans |
| 3. Credit rating agencies | Some products received higher ratings than their fundamentals justified; correlations were underestimated; "ratings shopping" by issuers may have biased ratings upward |
| 4. Investors | Highly accommodative monetary policy intensified the search for yield; banks kept contingent exposure to SIVs with high rollover risk |

### Risk retention and the market since the crisis

Under Section 941 of the Dodd-Frank Act, the SEC and US banking regulators finalized **risk retention** rules in 2014 (Section 15G of the Securities Exchange Act). A securitizer of asset-backed securities, including CLOs, must retain at least **5% of the credit risk** and may not hedge or otherwise transfer the retained risk. The rule gives securitizers "skin in the game," aligning their interests with investors'. In 2018 a US federal appeals court ruled that managers of open-market CLOs are not securitizers for this purpose, which exempted them from the rule.

:::example Risk retention
A sponsor issues a USD 600 million auto loan securitization under the US rules.

1. Minimum retained credit risk: \(0.05 \times 600 =\) USD 30 million.
2. The sponsor cannot buy CDS protection on, or otherwise hedge, that USD 30 million, so it loses money if the loans it originated perform badly.
:::

The securitization market has rebounded but remains a fraction of its pre-crisis size. MBS issued by US government agencies dominate outstanding volume, new issuance, and trading. Consumer ABS held up through the crisis and have grown since. CLOs revived as corporate borrowing expanded after 2012, because their structures are transparent to investors and their collateral is fairly easy to value. S&P projected global structured finance issuance of about USD 1 trillion for 2019, and 2018 issuance was near its 2003 level. Unfinished, uncertain regulation still slows the recovery. Demand for instruments that transfer credit risk efficiently has not gone away, and an OTD model that genuinely disperses credit risk can support financial stability.

## Practice questions

:::question
A bank buys CDS protection on USD 20 million of notional at a spread of 120 basis points. The reference entity later defaults with a recovery rate of 30%. What are the annual premium and the payment the bank receives after the default?
- [x] Premium USD 240,000; payment USD 14.0 million
- [ ] Premium USD 240,000; payment USD 6.0 million
- [ ] Premium USD 24,000; payment USD 14.0 million
- [ ] Premium USD 2.4 million; payment USD 20.0 million
---
Premium: \(0.0120 \times 20{,}000{,}000 = 240{,}000\) per year. Payment: \((1 - 0.30) \times 20{,}000{,}000 = 14{,}000{,}000\).

USD 6.0 million is the recovered amount, not the loss. USD 24,000 treats 120 basis points as 0.12%. USD 2.4 million treats it as 12%, and USD 20 million ignores recovery.
:::

:::question
Which of the following does not transfer credit risk out of a bank's loan book?
- [ ] Buying credit default swap protection on a borrower
- [ ] Securitizing a pool of corporate loans into a CLO
- [x] Entering an interest rate swap that converts fixed-rate loan income to floating
- [ ] Syndicating most of a large loan to other lenders
---
An interest rate swap changes the bank's exposure to interest rates; if the borrower defaults, the bank still bears the loss. A CDS passes default losses to the protection seller, securitization sells the loans and their credit risk to investors, and syndication spreads the loan among other lenders.
:::

:::question
A bank owes a counterparty USD 30 million on some trades, and the counterparty owes the bank USD 50 million on others. The counterparty goes bankrupt and unsecured claims recover 40%. What is the bank's loss without enforceable netting, and with it?
- [x] USD 30 million without netting; USD 12 million with netting
- [ ] USD 20 million without netting; USD 12 million with netting
- [ ] USD 30 million without netting; USD 20 million with netting
- [ ] USD 50 million without netting; USD 20 million with netting
---
Without netting, the bank pays its full USD 30 million to the bankrupt estate and recovers 40% of its USD 50 million claim (USD 20 million), losing USD 30 million. With netting, the positions offset to a net claim of USD 20 million, of which 40% (USD 8 million) is recovered, so the loss is USD 12 million.
:::

:::question
A USD 200 million loan pool backs an equity tranche covering losses from 0% to 5%, a mezzanine tranche from 5% to 20%, and a senior tranche from 20% to 100%. The pool loses USD 22 million. How is the loss allocated?
- [x] Equity USD 10 million; mezzanine USD 12 million; senior USD 0
- [ ] Equity USD 1.1 million; mezzanine USD 3.3 million; senior USD 17.6 million
- [ ] Equity USD 10 million; mezzanine USD 30 million; senior USD 0
- [ ] Equity USD 0; mezzanine USD 0; senior USD 22 million
---
The equity tranche is USD 10 million (5% of 200) and absorbs the first losses, so it is wiped out. The remaining USD 12 million falls on the mezzanine tranche (USD 30 million, 5% to 20%), a 40% loss. The senior tranche starts losing only after pool losses exceed 20% (USD 40 million).

Splitting the loss in proportion to tranche size ignores the waterfall. Wiping out the whole mezzanine tranche would need losses of USD 40 million. Allocating losses to the senior tranche first reverses the order of subordination.
:::

:::question
Compared with buy-and-hold lending, which risks does the originate-to-distribute model remove from the originating bank, and what incentive problem does it create?
- [x] The credit, price, and liquidity risk of the loans; a weaker incentive to underwrite carefully and monitor borrowers
- [ ] Interest rate risk only; an incentive to monitor borrowers too closely
- [ ] Credit risk only; an incentive to keep more loans on the balance sheet
- [ ] Operational risk; no incentive problem, because investors monitor the loans
---
Once loans are sold to an SPV, the originator no longer owns them, so it bears no credit or price risk on them, and it has turned illiquid loans into cash. Because the originator expects to sell the loans, it has less reason to underwrite them carefully or to monitor borrowers, a moral hazard that the risk retention rules address.
:::

:::question
Why did financial guarantees from monoline insurers largely disappear from new municipal bond and ABS issuance after the crisis?
- [x] Monolines had insured large amounts of ABS and private-label MBS; as those securities defaulted, the monolines were downgraded and could no longer lift a bond to AAA
- [ ] Regulators prohibited guarantees on municipal bonds
- [ ] Netting agreements made guarantees unnecessary for bond investors
- [ ] Credit default swaps cannot reference municipal bonds or ABS
---
A monoline's guarantee is worth only as much as the monoline's own credit. Guaranteeing a bond to AAA required a AAA-rated guarantor. Payments on defaulted structured products led to downgrades and failures, which destroyed the value of their guarantees.
:::

:::question
Under the US credit risk retention rules finalized in 2014, what must the sponsor of a USD 800 million asset-backed securitization do?
- [x] Retain at least USD 40 million of the credit risk and not hedge it
- [ ] Retain at least USD 40 million of the credit risk, which it may hedge with credit default swaps
- [ ] Retain at least USD 80 million of the credit risk without hedging
- [ ] Sell the entire USD 800 million to investors to remove conflicts of interest
---
The rules require securitizers to keep at least 5% of the credit risk (\(0.05 \times 800 = 40\) million) with no hedging or other transfer of that retained risk, so that the sponsor loses money if the assets it securitized perform badly.
:::

:::question
An oil producer pledges its crude oil inventory as collateral for a bank loan. Why is this collateral weaker protection than its current market value suggests?
- [x] The producer is most likely to default when oil prices fall, which is also when the collateral is worth least
- [ ] Crude oil cannot legally be pledged as loan collateral
- [ ] Collateral increases the bank's exposure to the borrower
- [ ] Physical commodities have no market value in a default
---
This is wrong-way risk: the borrower's probability of default and the value of the collateral are driven by the same factor. A fall in oil prices raises the chance of default and lowers the amount the bank can recover at the same time.
:::

:::question
Before 2007, how did many banks depart from the originate-to-distribute model?
- [x] They kept or bought large amounts of securitized mortgage risk, much of it in leveraged off-balance-sheet conduits and SIVs funded with short-term debt
- [ ] They stopped securitizing mortgages and held all loans to maturity
- [ ] They sold every tranche, including the equity tranche, to outside investors
- [ ] They required mortgage borrowers to post additional collateral
---
Instead of passing risk to capital market investors, banks acted as investors, holding securitized risk directly and through ABCP conduits and SIVs set up to avoid capital requirements. Those vehicles funded long-term assets with short-term paper, so when funding dried up the risk came back to the sponsoring banks.
:::

:::question
A lead bank arranges a USD 1 billion syndicated loan on a firm commitment basis and plans to keep 20% of it. Other investors take only USD 600 million. How much of the loan does the bank end up holding?
- [x] USD 400 million
- [ ] USD 200 million
- [ ] USD 0
- [ ] USD 600 million
---
Under a firm commitment the bank guarantees the borrower the full USD 1 billion and keeps whatever it cannot sell: \(1{,}000 - 600 = 400\) million, twice its planned USD 200 million share. Under a best efforts arrangement, the borrower would have received only the amount investors took plus the bank's own share.
:::

:::question
What is the main advantage of hedging a loan with a credit default swap instead of selling the loan?
- [x] The bank keeps the loan and the client relationship, and its funding is unaffected, while the default risk passes to the protection seller
- [ ] The hedge removes all counterparty risk from the position
- [ ] The hedge eliminates the loan's interest rate risk
- [ ] The bank receives the CDS spread as additional income
---
A CDS isolates credit risk from funding and relationship management: the loan stays on the books and the borrower need not know. The bank pays, rather than receives, the spread, it takes on counterparty exposure to the protection seller, and its interest rate exposure is unchanged.
:::

:::question
A corporate bond has a modified duration of 5 and trades at a spread of 180 basis points over Treasuries. The spread widens to 260 basis points while Treasury yields are unchanged. What is the approximate change in the bond's price?
- [x] −4.0%
- [ ] −0.4%
- [ ] −9.0%
- [ ] −13.0%
---
The spread widens by 80 basis points, or 0.80%. Price change \(\approx -5 \times 0.0080 = -4.0\%\), a loss from credit spread risk with no default.

−0.4% uses 0.08% for the spread change. −9.0% and −13.0% multiply duration by the whole starting or ending spread rather than by the change.
:::
